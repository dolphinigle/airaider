# ROUNDS 2 & 3 — give the writer the missing facts, and watch which questions come back

Same method as round 1 (`ROUND1_PROMPT_AUDIT.md`): fresh Opus writers, one real fixture each, two
files and nothing else, write the card then report on it. Nine writer-reports now, across three
rounds, none of whom could see each other.

Measured against `docs/CARD_GOLD_STANDARD.md`.

---

|---|---|---|---|---|

rather than from invention.** Both are exactly the fields the round-2 writers asked for by name.

## WHAT ROUND 2 FIXED, AND KEPT FIXED

**The known-new contract now holds.** Given the rule and the `who`, two of three writers produced
the gold standard's own construction unprompted:

> *"A merchant up from Millbrook has come to the fort gate looking for armed men, and he gives his
> name as Gundo."*
> *"A steward of a minor manor, Syndus, rode up to the fort's gate at dusk."*

Designation first, name inside the same sentence, the person acting. That is N2 closed — *on the
naming*. Note the cost one writer flagged: *"the appositive is the only construction that satisfies
all three constraints at once, so the rules effectively dictate the shape of every card's first
sentence. Sameness across cards is the predictable consequence."* Watch for it.

**Nothing coined.** 3/3 in both rounds: *"no name I did not receive"*.

**The boilerplate is gone and what replaced it is better.** With the stake rumour cut and the pay
reduced to a clause, the pay rides inside a character beat — *"Gundo watches your hands while he
sets his coin down"* — which its writer called *"the one line I'd keep unchanged."*

## WHY THE WHY STILL FAILS — now precisely, in two named ways

**`because` was the wrong field, and both round-2 writers said so independently:**

> *"`because` gives me his DEMAND but not his STAKE… I needed one dealt line of what he LOSES if
> this fails."* — B
> *"`because` is a MOTIVE, and a motive is not a stake."* — C

So round 3 dealt `stakeIfLost`. Where it was written well, it worked outright:

> *"If that feeding stops, the pass closes, and the fort goes into winter on what is already inside
> the walls."* — and its writer's verdict: ***"the one question cards usually lose is the one the
> input handed me pre-written."***

Where it failed, it failed for two specific, fixable reasons:

**(i) No causal joint.** *"the manor's year-money walked out of the door with her, and it is his own
neck if it does not walk back in."*

> *"A woodward is a forest-warden. How does a forest-warden walking into the trees take a manor's
> year of money with her? The card asserts a link it cannot explain… I could not fix it in the
> prose, because inventing the mechanism would have been inventing the plot."*

**(ii) The loss lands on a stranger.** *"a man who breaks that promise does not trade in Millbrook
again."*

> *"the loss is somebody else's business licence. Nothing in the card gives the boss a reason to care
> whether Gundo trades in Millbrook."*

**⇒ Two rules for whoever writes `stakeIfLost`:** it must carry the MECHANISM by which this person's
absence causes the loss, and the loss must be something the reader can feel — a neck, a roof, a
season — not a standing or a privilege.

## WHY THE STEEL STILL FAILS — and the sharpest proposal of the nine reports

`knownObstacle` (the obstacle's own `standsTo`, dealt at beat 1 as what the client openly knows)
answered the question outright on one fixture. On the other two it failed for reasons that name
their own fix:

- **noClient path:** the obstacle is routed through a client that `noClient` deletes. *"Every
  noClient beat-1 card in this game therefore ships without the field that makes it a mercenary card
  rather than an errand."*
- **A later step's danger:** *"The one interesting sentence on the card advertises a different
  card."* The grove earns steel — three beats from now. THIS step's friction is a mood.

> **The proposal, from writer B, which generalises past this saga:**
> *"every field on this payload describes the SAGA — the client, the prize, the goal, the standing
> obstacle — and only two describe the STEP, one of which is a mood. A card lives or dies on the
> step, so the step is where the dealt facts are thinnest and where I was forced to invent most.
> Making `stepFriction` carry an ACTOR rather than an ATMOSPHERE is the smallest change that moves a
> dealt fact into the part of the card that has none."*

Its worked example: `"The market has its reasons not to answer questions"` → `"Another buyer's men
have been at the market a week, paying vendors for the same answer and for silence after."` One
field, and it supplies the why (a clock — someone is ahead of you), the steel (a threat on THIS
ground), and a reason for the ask block to exist.

## THE FINDING THAT SETTLES THE PHASE

> ***"The dealt facts outperformed my writing everywhere they existed."*** — writer B, round 3

The two live sentences on that card were `arrival` and `tell`, *"barely touched"*. A writer with no
knowledge of this project's §0 lever hierarchy re-derived it from one afternoon of doing the job.

---

## PROVEN 9/9 — THE ARITHMETIC DOES NOT CLOSE

Every writer, every round, broke the sentence budget, and each one showed the arithmetic:

> *"client + `arrival`, the goal in one sentence, `stakeIfLost`, `knownObstacle`, the step's
> outcome-wanted, `stepFriction`, `pay`, and the `tell`. That is eight obligations for a
> seven-sentence ceiling. Any card obeying this beat-1 spec must break one of the two."*

This is not a wording problem and no rewording fixes it. Obligations must be **cut**, and the first
candidate is named: `bible.goal` duplicates the client's `want` verbatim in every fixture.

---

## PROVEN 9/9 — THE ASK BLOCK IS WRITTEN BLIND

Every writer, every round, guessed at the same things:

> *"Is `favored` a small nudge or does it decide the roll? Is `clashing` a penalty or a hard bar?"*
> *"if `clashing` is a hard veto, I have just banned every brawler from a slot that only involves
> talking in a market."*
> *"I do not know whether a required tag FILTERS the roster — on beat 1, an unfillable slot would be
> a serious failure, and I cannot tell from the prompt whether I just created one."*

Three writers reached for `requiredTag`, each unsure whether they had bricked the card. **The prompt
never says what these fields do.** One sentence of consequence would end it.

## STILL DEAD AFTER THREE ROUNDS

- **`rarity`** — *"used ONLY as permission to run long"*, then *"completely dead. Ignored."* 9/9.
- **`location`'s "elven"** — 3/3 in round 3 dropped the one piece of world-colour in the payload,
  each for the same reason: no legal sentence to put it in.
- **`bible.goal`** — duplicates the client's `want`.
- **The quarry's `want`** — the most interesting fact in two of three fixtures, barred from beat 1
  as hidden truth. *"The elf is a parcel."* *"She is his luggage."* Both writers volunteered it as
  the card's biggest loss.

## WHAT THE WRITERS SAY ABOUT THE PROSE, UNPROMPTED

All three round-3 writers were asked whether they would pick their own card off a board of five.
**None said yes.** The self-critiques are worth reading whole; the pattern:

- The live sentences are the dealt ones (`arrival`, `tell`, `stakeIfLost` where well-written).
- The dead sentences are the mandated ones — the goal restated, the task wrapped in a passive to
  avoid the imperative (*"eleven syllables of throat-clearing before the only new word"*), the
  scaffolding sentence built to carry the pay clause.
- The generic sentence is always the friction, because the friction is dealt as an atmosphere.
