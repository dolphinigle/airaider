# Craft literature: short interactive prose attached to a mechanic

Research compiled 2026-08-24. Scope: published, citable craft guidance on **short prose that a
player reads immediately before or immediately after a mechanical commitment** — storylet root
text, branch/choice text, result text, card flavour text, quest hooks.

Everything below is quoted from the source. Paraphrase is marked `[paraphrase]`. Vague/motivational
material was deliberately excluded. The final section lists only rules that **two or more
independent sources state**.

---

## 1. Failbetter Games — Fallen London Writer Guidelines (the internal guide, published verbatim)

### 1a. Part III — "Writing for Fallen London"
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-iii

**Word budgets (hard numbers, their house rule):**
> "**Write short**
> Root descriptions should not go longer than 30 words.
> Branch descriptions should not go longer than 20 words.
> Result descriptions should not go longer than 100 words
> There are exceptional circumstances in exceptional storylets, but we're talking a couple of cases per content commission."

**Show don't tell, operationalised:**
> "This is incredibly vague advice, but a fundamental element of FL writing is *evocative images and incident*. Be wary of putting words in the player's mouth, thoughts in their head, or feelings in their heart. Focus on the character's senses and what is observable to them. **Don't tell them they're scared; scare them.**"

**A named failure mode — the totalising establishing phrase:**
> "'All across London…' … We've overused 'all across London'. It's often a warning sign that you're telling, not showing. If you find yourself writing it, try focusing instead on specific examples of whatever's happening. **Who is doing it? Where? How?**"

**Dialogue / exposition:**
> "Direct speech must pass the say-this-shit test, and is better than reported speech. If you find yourself sliding into exposition, try putting the NPC in a state of distress, desperation, or vulnerability. It'll encourage them to get to the point and make the dialogue more characterful."
> (The "say-this-shit test" is Harrison Ford to George Lucas: *"You can write this shit, George, but you sure can't say it."*)

**Register/period voice as seasoning:**
> "Be judicious with Victorian style. A little goes a long way. **Treat it as seasoning, not an ingredient.**"

**Choice/branch text must name the action:**
> "A branch should almost always be a **clear in-character action**. It's great to put colour and jokes in there, but make sure the title, the description or — at the bare minimum — the button text makes clear **what the character will be doing if you click this option**."

**Result text scope — one action, not a montage:**
> "**Don't let results run away with you.** A result should describe **one action** (or a couple of closely-related ones) and their results. If a result has the character doing a string of things (make a disguise, infiltrate a gang, work out which of them stole your cheese, chase him down, hand him to the constables), it's time to simplify, or break the actions out into multiple branches."

**Passive voice:**
> "It has its uses ('London was stolen by bats'), but all else being equal, it can drain energy from prose. Often, you'll find yourself retreating into the passive voice when you're distant from the text. … Treat it as a reminder to get down there and evoke."

**Single-sentence paragraphs:**
> "Be careful, here. Don't make a single sentence a separate paragraph if the only reason is trying to control the reader's pace. **It comes across as precious.**"

**Clichés:**
> "You are condemning your sentence to be swallowed without chewing. Sometimes that *is* what you want — if you've got a lot of information to pack into a small space, it's a useful shortcut. But generally, we want to find arresting ways to give readers another angle of experience on our world.
> Particular ones to avoid: diaphanous gowns / brief lives burning brightly / black as pitch / it was quiet. Too quiet."

**Setting consistency constraints (the class of rule, not the instance):** weather, time-of-day and
player-body descriptors are banned because they conflict with a nonlinear, replayable, avatar-agnostic
frame:
> "It never more than drizzles in Fallen London. … It's always dark. You can't wake with the crack of dawn … **Use social events to express time instead** — the knockers-up rousing workers from their beds, factory bells signalling knocking off time, revellers stumbling home in the small hours."
> "We can't assume the player is male, female, wears skirts, wears trousers, etc. … Write around it. 'Sleeves' or 'hems' are probably safe."

**Pop culture references:** > "No."

### 1b. Part II — "Designing for Fallen London"
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/fallen-london-writer-guidelines-part-ii

> "**Quality parsimony** means using as few unique qualities as possible while maintaining clarity and stability. … **Two qualities tends to be the magic minimum number for any content with an engaging interactive structure** — otherwise it's a simplistic chain or a grind."

> "It's fine (often preferable) to hold off giving rewards for a while, then grant them in a chunk at the end of a content strand."

> "**Use quality changes to make non-player characters more tangible.** When you can see consequence from a character while interacting with them, it immediately makes them more tangible. … **Be cautious — a string of quality changes in a result makes each one less important.**"

Note on the "reader a year from now" test for design notes:
> "Imagine reading quality notes as if you were coming back to them a year from now … Now imagine a total stranger reading them a year from now. Now imagine the stranger has a short temper, an ice pick, and your address."

### 1c. "Narrative Snippets: Writing for Story Games"
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/narrative-snippets-writing-for-story-games

**Length again, and the reread argument:**
> "For most storylets, **a single sentence for root and branch descriptions and a paragraph for event text** will do fine. It's especially important to keep text short when the player will read a bit of text multiple times. They'll probably just skim it after the second time anyway."

**Success text vs failure text — an explicit asymmetry rule:**
> "The text is one of the major draws of StoryNexus games. **It's a good idea to get players used to the idea that text is a reward for play. So, you might want to make success text a bit longer or more interesting than failure text.**"

**Ceiling:**
> "Economy of prose is a virtue. **If you go over four or five paragraphs, you've definitely gone too far.**"

**Sentence-level:**
> "Your player is reading your text, but they're also playing a game. They may be only reading with half their attention, or skimming quickly. … **Keep your sentences simple. Use short, simple constructions. Don't write with a lot of semi-colons or parentheses or use endless clauses strung together with commas.**"

**What goes first — the inverted pyramid:**
> "**Put the important stuff first.** This is the cornerstone and first rule of journalistic writing, but it holds true for games too. Players have limited attention and often skim-read, so **shove the important stuff at them straight away. If you can't do that, put the important stuff in the title** so that they know what's coming."

### 1d. "Narrative Snippets: Parsimony" — *fires in the desert*
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/narrative-snippets-parsimony-2

> "**Fires in the desert.** This is Failbetter's term for using underspecified narratives for effect. The idea is that the storylets a creator writes are the fires. But the paths between those stories are dark — they're the player's own.
> … you should let players fill in the blanks between your stories. If your story says 'I was away for two weeks, and when I returned, I saw…', **there's no need to specify what the character was doing in the time away**. … So, there's no need to fill in every detail. **The player's imagination is your ally.**"

Structural corollary (against combinatorial gating):
> "Make sure that most of the characters can see most of the content. The structure to adopt is that of **a river rather than a tree**. … You want any one character to see 90% of your content, not 50% or 10%."

### 1e. "Narrative Snippets: Agency and Choices"
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/narrative-snippets-agency-and-choices

> "**Try not to tell the player what their character is feeling.** Describe what happens to the character, sure, but stay away from telling a player that their character is surprised or elated or scared. **It's the player's conception of their character that's important, rather than yours.**"

> "Don't fall into the trap of falling in love with another character, and having the player character around to witness someone else's adventures."

> "It's usually better to present **a series of five two-option choices rather than give ten options and have the character pick five times.** … once a player has picked the choice they want, it's no longer an interesting decision if they have the same choice straight away."

> "It's hard to lay out complex situations in branch text." — i.e. for a big decision, **set up the situation in one storylet and put the choice in the next.**

### 1f. "Narrative Snippets: Difficulty, Rewards and Punishment"
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/narrative-snippets-difficulty-rewards-and-punishment

**The single most transferable rule for dice-resolved prose:**
> "**Talking about Difficulty.** When you have challenges on qualities … **don't talk about how hard the challenge is in the surrounding fiction.** If you talk about the 'desperate, painful struggle', it will be odd for the player whose character has lots of the relevant quality and a 100% chance of succeeding.
> Characters will often end up with qualities at levels you didn't expect. … So, **don't reference how hard a problem was, just that there was a success or a failure.** If you want to emphasise how difficult something is, it's better to give it a cost."

**On punishment:**
> "Punish characters — they'll love you for it. … **They won't remember when you took a few actions of progress away from them, but they'll remember being thrown into the weasel pits.**"

**On trophies:**
> "Another sort of reward is more like a souvenir or trophy — a mark that the character has done something in particular, like Hero of Broad Street or Silver-Tongued Devil. … **But don't go overboard — they become less valuable if you shower a character with them.**"

### 1g. "Narrative Snippets: Pacing" — repeatable content
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/narrative-snippets-pacing

> "**You need to keep it short** — the player [is] going to skim over text after a few goes through anyway … You should keep the fiction reasonably generic. That doesn't mean boring, but it needs to be something that a player could do twenty times. **So, stealing a diamond or meeting a wolf, but not stealing the world's biggest diamond or meeting Lupius, father of all wolves.**"

> "Give the player goals. Or let the player choose from a set of goals. And then **tell the player how far along they are** in reaching those goals."

### 1h. Yasmeen Khan — "Points of light, pools of shadow" (Echo Bazaar house style, parts I & II)
Sources: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/points-of-light-pools-of-shadow-part-i
· https://www.failbettergames.com/news/points-of-light-pools-of-shadow-part-ii

**The target length, stated as a target rather than a cap:**
> "Very tiny amounts of text aren't rich enough to support this, and very large blocks of text just invite the eye to skip over them altogether. **We aim for exactly short enough.**"

**Rule 3 — one concrete detail carries the whole scene:**
> "Colour, incident and detail are the keys to making the text rewardingly rereadable. … **Less is more. We want to aspire to Rembrandt or Vermeer, say, not Van Gogh or Picasso.**"
> "For example, we might use an image of **red threads of firelight playing on a glass of cognac in a devil's hand.** … it creates a much more vivid sense of place and atmosphere than a general description of a warm stuffy room with a fire crackling in the grate … **the rest of the room, the warmth, the furniture, the shadow, is already there, wrapped in that one detail.** … we want to make sure that the player carries the burden of shaping what's in the shadows that fill 90% of the canvas."

**Anchor in *where/how*, never in *when*:**
> "**We can't specify *when* in the player's own narrative a scene is taking place or how many times it's happened to them before. But we can anchor the scene in details of *where* or *how* it takes place.**"

**Hedging as a deliberate device:**
> "So we probably wouldn't say 'No one has ever returned safely from the Pale Wastes'. Instead, we'd probably go for '…they *say* few, if any, have survived the Pale Wastes'. This version allows for things to change in the past or in the future. **It creates ambiguity — how reliable are 'they'? — which in turn creates space for players to make their own decisions** … But we can only do this because the solid foundations are there."

**Passive voice, with the interactive-specific reason:**
> "For an interactive narrative, it can be tempting to use them for effect, to make the reader the subject at the centre of the sentence — 'you are led through the garden' … But on reading, it has the opposite effect. **A passive verb takes the illusion of control away from the reader.** When you don't even know who's leading you through the garden, you're not in control."

**Rule 4 — simple vocabulary, period flavour via nouns not syntax:**
> "**Says rather than utters. Shouts, not vociferises. Argues instead of expostulates.** … We use simple English — and we add a few twists. … We scatter the stage with **Victorian whatnots — hansom cabs, cruet sets, tallboys and so forth.** … **Simple structures with just enough interesting words.**"
> "…a detail like a ticking grandfather clock or a dusty velvet curtain … **They can carry a whole atmosphere by themselves.**"

**NAMES — the most directly transferable rule in the corpus:**
> "**We give a lot of characters 'generic' names — the Scarred Naturalist, the Implacable Detective** … **Characters without individual names are more universal, less specific, less prone to continuity problems. It's more believable and immersive to have each player encounter their 'own' Keen-eyed Lapidary.**"
> "We have a scale by which some are more generic than others … **A Swivel-Eyed Patriot** you encounter could be one of many similar characters … **The Implacable Detective** is less mutable — there's a strong sense that there's only one of her, but she has a tangential, facilitative function. **She brings stories to you, rather than takes part in them.** … At the far end of the scale, **we do name characters who are a definite and individual part of the world — Mrs Plenty, Sinning Jenny, Dr Schlomo.**"
> "Sometimes, we wish we had given them names when their roles grow. Sometimes, we're very glad we didn't. It's not an exact science."

**Continuity-safe dialogue formulas:**
> "A character can say something like **'Whatever happened between us in the past, business is business'** or **'Perhaps you have already met so-and-so? Even if you have, I must remind you…'** This allows continuity within storylines in which the player may have chosen between betraying or saving someone."

**Interpretation over explanation:**
> "There's a lot more we can do with direct speech to suggest ambiguities rather than spell them out. **We want you to interpret characters' motivations rather than offer you possible explanations.**"

### 1i. "Choice, Complicity and Consequence" (Failbetter's core vocabulary)
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/choice-complicity-and-consequence

> "**Choice** is your experience immediately before a decision; **complicity** the experience of acting on that decision; and **consequence** the experience of what happens later."
> "**Choice** is that sense of fulcrum-shift you get when you consider a decision you care about."
> "Of the three, **consequence is the most expensive** — and it gets more expensive *very* quickly as you add more complexity. … But **twenty variant endings aren't ten times as good as two variant endings** — not if the complicity you felt, and the choices you made, were poorly implemented."

### 1j. Nigel Evans — "Don't Poke That: Narrative Engineering Principles"
Source: https://www.failbettergames.com/news/dont-poke-that-narrative-engineering-principles-part-1

> "**Show them the Implements.** … If we're making you suffer, **you want to know how long you're going to be up on that cross.** It's no fun to just be up there and not know when we're going to let you down. So we're making a point in our more robustly unsympathetic content **to let you know just how much more of this shit you're going to have to take.**"

## 2. Emily Short

### 2a. "The Prose Medium and IF" (her core prose-craft essay)
Source: https://emshort.blog/how-to-play/writing-if/my-articles/the-prose-medium-and-if/

**Every line is a *Suggester* — text is instruction disguised as description:**
> "**All text in interactive fiction is at least potentially a direction to the player**, and the author's willingness to direct the player is a critical part of making a game accessible."
> Her examples — *"Something glints at the bottom of the lake."* / *"Through the fragile surface of the glass ball, you make out the wavy outlines of a key."* — "direct the player to examine, to smash, to kiss. (**Nick Montfort's dissertation refers to this as the Suggester role of IF text.**)"
> "…it just consists of **a willingness to foreground in description the functional aspects of an object, drawing the player's attention gently toward those features of something that are most likely to be useful.**"

**Economy, with the exchange rate spelled out:**
> "I *am* arguing for a kind of writing that avoids detail for detail's sake. … **if we have no such purpose in mind, and the details have no bearing on game play, they are probably best omitted. This is a medium that rewards restraint.**"
> "Some of the most effective writers of mood create their effect **not with a large number of common details (the flowers are red, the door is yellow, etc) but with a small number of very particular ones**; and I think that that is especially true in IF."
> "**Words in interactive fiction *individually* carry more weight than they carry in static prose**, if only because of the amount of attention we demand the player give to each one."
> On P.D. James-style description: "**They'd need to be shortened and focused, because each sentence would do the work of three or four sentences in the static prose version. In this respect IF is closer to poetry than to conventional prose: it is worth taking more time to select fewer words, because each one will be inspected through a jeweler's loupe.**"

**Confidence — the opening-line rule:**
> "**The first page of a novel that sells presents itself with utter assurance. It knows what it is going to be about, it sets its tone, and it directs the reader's attention to something interesting. It does not dither. It does not ramble.** … The prose can be leaden, the characters rice-paper thin, but there will still be something authoritative and fearless about the opening."
> "This kind of contract with the player is just as important in IF, or even more so. **We rely on the player's trust and confidence in the author; without it, the player won't experiment as much.**"

**Don't promise in prose what the system won't honour:**
> "…**avoiding any commitments in prose that you're not willing to honor in the world model.**"

### 2b. "Storylets: You Want Them"
Source: https://emshort.blog/2019/11/29/storylets-you-want-them/

Definition (the canonical one):
> "there is a piece of content … there are **prerequisites** that determine when the content can play … there are **effects** on the world state that result after the content has played"

On what you give up when content is gated on exact history:
> "At this point, you're not getting most of the value of using storylets — the robust ability to add things later, the possibility of making stories interlock in interesting ways, the design discipline of generalizing about how your narrative space works."

### 2c. "Pacing Storylet Structures"
Source: https://emshort.blog/2020/01/21/pacing-storylet-structures/

> "**Areas where the player has a lot of freedom tend to feel less intense; more linear, constrained sections are good for climactic moments. Alternating the two keeps the experience from feeling too stale.**"

On a cost-linked choice before a risk scene:
> "**I'm effectively asking her to set some mechanical stakes of her own and place a bet about how much an easy success later is going to matter to her.**"

On where the reused/generic content may sit:
> "Putting this travel segment right before the big confrontation gives us a little time to build anticipation and create a sense of occasion — after the stakes for this particular story have already been set. **It wouldn't have been so satisfying as the first step of the story, though. The story's up-front hook needs to be new content.**"

### 2d. "Mailbag: Development Process for Storylet-based IF"
Source: https://emshort.blog/2020/02/18/mailbag-development-process-for-storylet-based-interactive-fiction/

On naming the stat/quality — evocativeness vs reusability:
> "**A quality like 'Personal Growth' or 'Dramatic Tension' or 'Ennui' is way more transferable between different stories — but way less evocative — than a quality like 'Number of Times You Fed the Chameleon.'**"

### 2e. "Small-Scale Structures in CYOA" — micro-patterns for a single decision
Source: https://emshort.blog/2016/11/05/small-scale-structures-in-cyoa/

> "**Confirmation-required Choice.** One of the things Jon [Ingold] talks about sometimes is the use of text to let the player opt in to doing something profoundly stupid, through a series of escalating choices. *Are you sure you want to do this? It looks like the monster is getting angrier. Are you still sure you want to attack? Yes? You notice that the monster's bite is poisonous.* … **Once the player has opted in multiple times, it's really on their own head if they wind up in a situation with a combat roll that wipes them out. This expands what would otherwise be a binary decision into an experience with more tension**; it also tends to work well in cases where there's one dangerous-but-interesting option and one safe-but-bland option."

### 2f. "Choice Poetics (Peter Mawhorter)" — the named failure modes of a choice
Source: https://emshort.blog/2019/04/09/choice-poetics-peter-mawhorter/

Choice anatomy:
> "He starts by anatomizing choices, breaking them down into **framing** (what comes before the choice and helps us understand what it's going to be); **the choice option texts themselves**; and **outcomes**. … **I've seen some breakdowns of choice that don't look enough at the framing of choice**, so it's useful to have that called out."

Named bad-choice types:
> "**'blind choice'** (the infamous 'go left or go right' choice where **the player has no framing information to establish the stakes of the choice**); **'false choice,'** where it looks like there's going to be a branch in the narrative consequent on your action, but in fact there isn't; **'dead-end option'** … **'dilemmas' as opposed to 'flavor choices'**; **'unchoices' where you have only one option**."

Mawhorter's formal definitions (quoted by Short):
> "For **'obvious'** choices, there should be exactly one option which 'achieves' a goal and does not 'fail' any, while no other option should 'achieve' any goal, and all other options should at least 'threaten' a goal.
> For **'relaxed'** choices, each option must at least 'enable' or 'achieve' a goal, and none may 'threaten' or 'fail' any goals. Additionally, the stakes should be low.
> For **'dilemma'** choices, all options should 'threaten' if not 'fail' a goal, and those goals should all have the same priority. No option should 'achieve' any goal."

An observation directly relevant to generated option text:
> "**Human-written option text often makes one of the options a punch line, and players tend to be drawn to those** — but it is trickier to emulate that effect in a generative system like this."

On uncertainty being *stated* (the Fallen London model):
> "…in *Fallen London*, where **the player is explicitly told how likely it is that a given attempt will succeed**: Phrases like 'A *chancy* challenge for your Persuasive quality' **quantify the uncertainty the player is facing**."
> "**Committing to a high-risk choice at a high-stakes moment is, for some players, a way to play for maximum drama, and one might want the outcome text to reflect the nail-biting nature of that interaction.**"

## 3. inkle / Jon Ingold

### 3a. "The Problem of Failure" (the text of an unheld GDC 2019 talk)
Source: https://medium.com/@inklestudios/the-problem-of-failure-518ec9c1c53e

**What a failure/partial-success text is *for*:**
> "…in *Die Hard*, we know for a fact that McClane won't be killed. But he might well be shot — he might start bleeding, causing him to leave a trail as he moves. He might lose the use of one hand, making it harder to climb. Stray bullets might cause an electrical fire. And as audiences, **we're watching because we expect there will be *some consequence* to the drama, and *we don't yet know what it'll be*.**"
> "**In action and adventure stories, it's common for the hero to face a challenge and *neither succeed nor fail*, but rather get through at a cost, with that cost setting up the next beat of the story.**"

**Against the binary:**
> "…both of these solutions come with an assumption which is unique to games: **that *failure is a bad thing*.**"
> "Is there space for games where the player does not expect to *succeed* but where **failure itself is interesting**? Can we build games that encourage you to **fail forwards**, careering from disaster to disaster …? Can we make games that are designed around **squeaking through**?"

**The design goal, stated as a rule:**
> "Narratively, one of our goals as designers is to **sustain the experience of failing for as long as possible. If you can keep the player failing-but-not-failed, you have a player who is still trying, still investing, still caring. If they win, or lose, the matter is finished; but if there's still *life* there is still *hope*.**"

### 3b. Haywire Magazine — "Wordplay: Jon Ingold" (Joe Köller)
Source: https://haywiremag.com/features/wordplay-jon-ingold/

**The measured finding on passage length — the most concrete data point in this corpus:**
> "In *Frankenstein* … the pages of text were quite long. **We found that just that — having three or four paragraphs to read before the next choice came along — caused a significant tail-off in people's interest in reading. So for *Sorcery!* we broke everything up into ruthlessly short chunks, and you can see the effect on people playing. They read without meaning to.**"

> "It's the same as the way writers for magazines work, **ensuring everything they print is highly consumable**. … **You make the reading too much like hard work, and you'll have a thousand players checking Twitter instead.**"

> "**Text is a visual medium.** I hadn't thought of that idea until I came to write my GDC talk, but having struck on it, it seems obviously true. Same reason I break long emails into paragraphs — you'd never read them if I didn't."

### 3c. Narrative News interview, Apr 2026 (Rose Behar)
Source: https://narrativenews.substack.com/p/inkles-jon-ingold-on-the-craft-of

**Length as an attention contract:**
> "I think the reality, if we're honest, is it's mostly about **not overestimating the attention span of your audience**."
> "There's a kind of balance when you're writing anything … **between how much you can convey and how much trust you've earned from the reader in the first place. I think that balance is quite often somewhat more conservative than people realize.**"
> "**Text is something we engage with primarily on a visual level first. You see a wall of text, and your brain says that's going to take me about this long to read, and that's before you actually go into the words.**"
> "**The number of lines is hugely important to me** because it's about the rhythm and how much you're asking of the reader in this given moment. I never stop thinking about pacing, timing, momentum, and that kind of sense of **'have I earned the player's attention in this paragraph?'**"

**The improv model of a beat — offer *just enough*:**
> "If you're doing an improv scene with someone, **you can't just start talking for 10 minutes and leave the other person hanging. That's extremely uncool. But you also can't offer nothing. You have to offer just enough to throw the Frisbee back to them.** … the fact that it keeps trading back and forth **is the delightful aspect of it, not actually the virtuosic performance of the writer**."

**What a moment before commitment should feel like:**
> "I get the most excited when you're in a conversation with a character in a game, you're five or six choices deep, and then a prompt will come up where **you really want a particular outcome**. It's **being on the cusp of the moment** that I find really exciting."
> "When someone is enjoying a game of blackjack, or a game of poker, they are feeling exactly the same feeling. **I want this card to be an ace. I hope it's an ace. I think it's going to be an ace. And that moment when you turn the card over and you find out whether it is or not.**"

### 3d. "Sparkling Dialogue" (AdventureX 2018) — notes by Robert Yang
Source: https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html
(Yang's own caveat: "This post isn't a transcript of Ingold's talk. It's a summary with my interpretations.")

**The failure mode named: repetition-as-information-delivery.** Ingold's worked example is an
*Assassin's Creed Odyssey* scene that repeats the merchant's name and location three times each.
> "**this scene doesn't go anywhere. It just keeps repeating the same information over and over** … **What is this scene for, dramatically? It's not doing any *dramatic work*.**"

**Named failure mode: the non-choice.**
> "Ingold points to '**What do you think?**' as the classic video game '**they've given me a choice!**' type of **non-choice, a meaningless option for the sake of offering options to the player. It's so random, it has no context. This is what you say when you can't think of anything else to say.**"

**Named failure mode: lore review no one in-world would need.**
> "**Why is Rachel explaining what blade runners are? Everyone in this fictional world already knows what blade runners are. Game writers do this expository lore review stuff all the time, and it always feels bad.**"

**The three-option template:**
> "**Ingold usually likes offering 3 types of choices. (Two is too binary as if there's a correct choice, and four lacks focus.)** … He calls this pattern **Accept / Reject / Deflect**:
> **Accept:** follow the current topic, answer their question, be cooperative and relevant.
> **Reject:** go on the attack, you've had enough of this nonsense.
> **Deflect:** escape the confrontation or change subject, like a 'joke option' but not a joke so it doesn't break the tone of the scene."

**Trapdoors:**
> "You should also offer the player a '**trapdoor**' to exit the loop, and escalate the scene to continue forward. … this trapdoor is useful for players who want to skip ahead, and it's also a valid thing to express in a conversation. ('A: Do you like our owl? B: I'm not here to talk about owls.')"

**Structure as characterisation:**
> "**Loops slow down dialogue, trapdoors speed up dialogue, and both of those patterns tell you about the characters' mood and tone. Your use of branching structure is a form of characterization.** … **Branching is action, action is character, character is drama.**"

**Ingold's own disclaimer, worth keeping:** > "as with all writing advice, it's bullshit, but hopefully it's useful bullshit."

### 3e. "Introduction to Ink" (Jon Ingold, Wireframe)
Source: https://medium.com/game-writing-guide/introduction-to-ink-3e6c224865f8

> "**By default, ink will repeat the text of the choice into the flow of the story, but that — like most things! — can be changed with a bit more markup.**"
(The ink square-bracket syntax exists specifically so the *choice* text and the *resulting* prose can
differ — e.g. choice `"Ah."` produces output `"Ah," I replied`. Source for the design rationale:
https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/open-sourcing-80-days-narrative-scripting-language-ink)

## 4. Choice of Games — house rules for choice-based prose

### 4a. Rebecca Slitt, "How to Write Intentional Choices" (the single most on-point piece for a commit-resources card)
Source: https://www.choiceofgames.com/2016/12/how-to-write-intentional-choices/

> "**In order for a player to feel emotionally invested in their choices, they have to know why they're choosing it, and they have to have some idea of what the consequences of that choice might be.**"

> "A standard practice in old choose-a-path gamebooks was to offer the options 'Go right' or 'Go left' without any more information about what the reader might find in each direction. The reader would have to choose randomly. **Then, they'd probably fall off a cliff.**"

**The three things to signal *before* the choice:**
> "In practical terms, this means that **before a choice, you should try to signal:
> — the potential story results for each option
> — which stats might be tested, if the option leads to a stat test
> — the relative difficulty of each option, if some options are harder than others.**"

> "So intentionality is important in general. **It's especially important in a few particular circumstances. Specifically, when the potential consequences of an option are negative; and when different options within the same choice have different difficulty levels.**"

Her worked example from *Mecha Ace* shows framing prose that leaks stat + difficulty without naming
numbers — "if you are fast enough—or lucky enough—you might even be able to withdraw" (tests Speed);
"provided you kept calm" (tests Willpower); "with a pilot as skilled as Hawkins" (tests Piloting):
> "**The text clarifies the potential risks and rewards of each option, shows the relative difficulty of each option, and hints at the stats that might be tested for each one** — in other words, it allows the player to make the choice with full intentionality."

> "**In conclusion, one of the best ways to make your choices meaningful is to make them intentional. To do that, give clues in the text about the potential story effects and stat effects of each option, and indicate when one option is easier or harder than the others.**"

### 4b. "How We Judge a Good Game — Part 2" (the option-writing rubric)
Source: https://www.choiceofgames.com/2017/03/how-we-judge-a-good-game-part-2/

**The clause test for option text — the baseball-cap rule:**
> "**When reading the choice, a player should have at least a rough estimate of what the outcomes of the different options are going to be.** … For example, **'I put on a baseball cap' doesn't tell the player much, while 'I put on a baseball cap because it looks good on me', or 'I put on a baseball cap because I can pull it down to hide my face' indicate what kind of stat they may be raising.**"

**Against the null option:**
> "Most, or even all, choices should have **three or more options, none of which should be 'do nothing' options.** If the player can choose to not do anything, **it should only be in direct pursuit of a goal.** For example, 'I keep silent in order to pressure the culprit into confessing on their own' is a perfectly valid option, while 'I'll wait and see, because that will let me get more information' is a little more suspect (why not just give the player the information without them asking?), and **'I don't join the adventure' should not ever be an option.**"

**Balance:**
> "**No option or path should be better or worse than any other** — that means both in terms of how interesting the decision you're offering the player is, and in terms of the stat effects each option has."

**Stat semantics must be stable across the game (a consistency rule for generated text):**
> "**if the option 'I put on a baseball cap' raises Charisma in Ch 3, the same option in Ch 6 shouldn't raise Stealth instead.**"

**On outcome text after a resolution:**
> "**The player should always understand why they reached the ending that they did. Having an ending come from nowhere, or an ending which feels like it's not accounting for something leaves a lasting bad impression.**"
> "**Endings that ignore stats** (e.g. no matter your 'swordfighting' skill you lose the duel at the end to build tension), **insert a standard piece of epilogue which doesn't work** (e.g. you always settle down to a quiet life after the adventure, even if you were a bloodthirsty barbarian during it), **or come out of nowhere** … **are not satisfying. … if a player feels like it assigns qualities or actions to them that don't fit, it detracts from any feeling of satisfaction.**"
> "There should also be something **dramatic and satisfying about every ending, even if the ending is unhappy.** … **endings where a player simply fails everything should not appear.**"

### 4c. "How We Judge a Good Game — Part 3" + Mary Duffy, "How to Edit Yourself"
Sources: https://www.choiceofgames.com/2017/04/how-we-judge-a-good-game-part-3/
· https://www.choiceofgames.com/2018/01/how-to-edit-yourself/

**House POV rule:** > "**second person games should use first person options**" (narration is "you", the
option the player clicks is "I").

**The line-level cut test (their editor, quoting Vonnegut):**
> "**The dictum of fiction is: 'Does this sentence reveal character or move the plot forward?' If it doesn't, cut it.**" — Mary Duffy
> "**Kurt Vonnegut's dictum that each sentence should either reveal character or advance the plot is particularly good.**"

**Named tic-words to search-and-destroy:**
> "**Do you use the word 'realize' or 'suddenly,' or the phrase 'and then' to exhaustion? Find them, replace them.**"
> "Reading aloud will bring to light infelicities like **ambiguous antecedents ('Wait, who?'), using the same word twice in succession ('Oh, I started the last paragraph with "suddenly"')**."

**On the pitch/hook (transferable to a card's opening line):**
> "**Try to condense your game down [to] a single sentence, and see if it's both understandable, and still feels interesting. If it's extremely easy to explain your entire game in a single sentence, or you can do it by just pointing to a genre or another story, it's possible your game may be lacking originality. If you simply can't, then your concept might need to be simplified a bit.**"
> "**Games with a gripping premise make readers curious (without confusing them!)**"

**Judges on what makes them keep going:**
> Dan: "**A good story is like a good joke. The ending has to be surprising but inevitable in hindsight.**"
> Jason: "**The thing that I want is to not be asked the same question repeatedly. I want the choices to be in different registers; about different plotlines; to be at cross-purposes.**"
> Becky: "**Does the story make me feel something? Does it make me want to know what happens next?**"

### 4d. Dan Fabulich, "By the Numbers"
Source: https://www.choiceofgames.com/2011/07/by-the-numbers-how-to-write-a-long-interactive-novel-that-doesnt-suck/

> "**delayed branching**, in which earlier choices don't branch the story right away; instead, they **determine the outcome of later decisions**." — with the *Choice of the Dragon* example where Brutality wins the fair duel and Finesse wins the trap, i.e. **the same later choice reads differently depending on prior stats.**

## 5. Sam Kabo Ashwell — "Standard Patterns in Choice-Based Games"
Source: https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/

Useful mainly as shared vocabulary, plus the tone implication of each shape:

> "**Time Cave.** … broad rather than long. … The player has velocity but little grasp, vast freedom but little ability to comprehend it."
> "**Gauntlet.** Long rather than broad, gauntlets have a relatively linear central thread, pruned by branches which end in death, backtracking, or quick rejoining. … the gauntlet creates **an atmosphere of a hazardous, difficult or constrained world**."
> "**Branch and Bottleneck.** The game branches, but the branches regularly rejoin … **To avoid obliterating the effect of past choices, branch-and-bottleneck structures almost always rely on heavy use of state-tracking.** … most often used to reflect **the growth of the player-character**."

## 6. Card flavour text (Magic: the Gathering and CCGs) — the closest analogue form

### 6a. Mark Rosewater, "The Write Stuff" (Making Magic, 2002)
Source: https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/write-stuff-2002-03-18

> "**One of the challenges of writing flavor text is trying to get a big idea conveyed in as few words as possible. Besides being concise, flavor text also needs to have some rhythm and flow (it needs to sound nice).**"
> On *Luminous Guardian* ("There is no victory without virtue."): "**it captures a nice flavor of white in six words, and it does so with alliteration.**"
> "**Telling parables in flavor text is a challenge as I had to tell a full story in two or three lines of text.**"
> On writing a rallying line: "**For purpose of rhythm, I used a speech with three beats. (In the world of writing, this is known as the Rule of Three.)**"
> On character voice: "the flavor text team tried an experiment. **To give a consistent voice to the major *Magic* characters, each crew member was assigned to a different writer.**"
> On why one line landed: "**If *Tempest* were a movie, I felt that this was the kind of line people would quote.**"
> On comedy: "**comedy was all about juxtaposition. That is, comedy was about connecting two things that aren't normally connected.**"

Rosewater's follow-up column (https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/making-magic/add-text-flavor-2002-03-25)
reproduces the strongest published criticism of joke-forward flavour text — worth keeping as the
counterweight:
> "**Cheap, broad 'humor' and the casual tone of modern flavor text is killing the once wonderful tone** … **These annoying characters make the flavor text impersonal and monotonous. They take the focus off the players and thrust it onto a bunch of unlikable, one-dimensional characters.**" — Will Mistretta, quoted at length by Rosewater

### 6b. "The Pro's Guide To CCG Flavor Text Writing" (Wonders CCG, by a working CCG lead designer)
Source: https://www.wondersccg.com/2024/03/01/the-pros-guide-to-ccg-flavor-text-writing/

**The budget:** > "**Each card has a limit, such as '100 characters' to work within.**"

**Three rules, with worked bad/good pairs:**
> "**1. Show, don't tell.** Describe the results of what's happening, not what's happening itself. This can be challenging when you are forced to be brief.
> Bad: *He seethed with contempt and incredible demonic anger.*
> Good: *His lips curled and fists clenched. 'You utter fool.' He snarled.*
> Good: *No mortal had ever received that gaze and lived to see the next sunrise.*"
> "**2. Be evocative, emotional, or surprising.** Engage the senses or emotions in some way. Or approach the situation from an unexpected source.
> Bad: *The sun shone on the meadow as nature recovered from the fire.*
> Good: *Chirping insects filled the air and the scent of grass drifted above the ash.*"
> "**3. Be true.** Do a reality check that the text fits with the world, style, and characters."

**Additional rules:**
> "**Leave something to the imagination. Just describe a small part of what's happening and let the reader fill in the rest.** Example: *With stabbing claws, the first bat latched on his face. Then a second and a third. **He couldn't see what happened next.***"
> "**Quips, cute sayings, jokes and reversals can be great, but use them sparingly. Fun wordplay is memorable, but a little goes a long way.**"
> "**Quotes are good if it's clear who's saying them and what the context is. It's best to use them on characters so you learn something about the speaker.** Example: *'Only sixteen heads chopped today. Gonna bring down my average.'*"
> "**Cycles or related cards can have related flavor text, filling in parts of the lore.** … *The city of power, whose wealth crushes those in the way.* / *The city of markets, whose winds bring travelers from unheard-of lands.* / *The city of iron, whose engines are fired by chained dragons.*"
> "**Advice or warnings, as if from a guidebook, can be another good option if used sparingly.** Example: *'First you feel the rumble. Then you hear the crashing. Then you start running.'*"
> "**Add a small twist or surprising snippet to what the player already knows about the card.** Example (on a venomous insect): *'One sting is enough to kill a person. **And the undertaker if he doesn't use gloves.**'*"

**Process note (relevant to any generate-N-and-pick pipeline):**
> "**Each writer is assigned a bunch of cards and writes multiple submissions for each. Different people write for the same card, making the selection process competitive. The creative team or team lead reviews submissions and selects the best one.**"

### 6c. MTG Wiki — flavour-text policy
Source: https://mtg.wiki/page/Flavor_text
> "**Real-world quotations (mostly literature, the bible and so on) were a thing in early sets, but nowadays they are mostly avoided.**"
> "**A few versions of iconic cards … may have no flavor text, even though there is room for it, as flavor text could be seen as detracting from the overall aesthetic of these staples.**" — i.e. *sometimes the right amount of flavour text is none.*

## 7. Alexis Kennedy — writing card-sized text for a mechanic (Cultist Simulator)
Source: https://medium.com/sex-lies-and-videogames/alexis-kennedy-on-the-cultist-simulator-the-way-through-the-wood-part-1-c53ca6a39e0

**The three jobs of a one-line card text, stated together:**
> "Whenever a player examined an object, or got a result, the prototype would give them **a pithy or enigmatic line of text**. Try fasting, and you'd read *'When your mouth waters, when your vision swims, you see the red cup; and now and then it will speak to you.'* …
> So, **each piece of text was — hopefully! — a tiny reward in itself — long enough to engage attention, but not long enough to feel like homework. More than that, though, the snippets were fragments of a larger story. They also provided clues towards the underlying system of the game — trails of breadcrumbs that the player could follow.**"

**On what makes a resolving timer *interesting* to wait for** (three factors, quoted in condensed form):
1. the resource is scarce and you care that you got it;
2. "**the possibility of other, different, *somethings*, for either curiosity or profit. If an interaction gave you an *Atlas of Dreams* or a *murmuring amulet*, that immediately suggested other possible interactions**";
3. "**words. Story, narrative, lore.**"

**On the felt state he was designing for:**
> "**you were *always waiting for something interesting to happen* — and you always knew exactly how long that something was going to take before it arrived. It put me immediately in a state of constant pleasant anticipation.**"

## 8. MMO / RPG quest text
Source (GDC Austin 2009, Tracy Seamster of ZeniMax Online + Steve Danuser of 38 Studios, reported by Game Developer):
https://www.gamedeveloper.com/pc/gdc-austin-writing-for-mmos-you-re-doing-it-wrong

**The blunt premise:**
> "**No one wants to read in MMOs.** As much as writers want to think their quest prose is for the ages, reading is not a popular activity in online gaming."
> "Designers can spend hours working on a quest description, looking for just the right turn of phrase. **Because players are more interested in acting than reading, though, quests are a very poor way to express game story.**"

**Their fix — cut to the interesting beat:**
> On an *EverQuest 2* time-travel quest: "**it took 27 pieces of quest text to get the player back in time. No one remembers the text, they note. Why not just skip to the good part with a few well-written quips, and let the player just get on with it?**"

**Broad strokes beat nuance when attention is short:**
> "**if a story is amazing in the writer's head, but the players can't understand it, that's wasted work.** She noted that MMOs move too quickly for players to sit around 'noodling' about details. **Offering story in broad strokes may not feel as nuanced, but it will be far better understood.**"

**Against the undifferentiated quest board:**
> "Danuser actually argued against what's been called '**the Christmas tree effect**', when a player stumbles upon a quest hub with a dozen or more new quests available. **Epic quests and meaningless scut-work is lumped together in that scenario, washing out and trivializing content.**"

**A tone document as the artefact that keeps a many-writer world consistent:**
> "**The tone document … should allow writers to understand the range of expression the game allows. A good tone document will clarify for writers what kinds of stories should be in the world.**"

## 9. Curiosity / the information gap (the academic underpinning of "why commit?")

**George Loewenstein, "The Psychology of Curiosity: A Review and Reinterpretation," *Psychological Bulletin* 116(1), 1994.**
> Curiosity arises "**when attention becomes focused on a gap in one's knowledge. Such information gaps produce the feeling of deprivation labeled curiosity.**"
> Curiosity is "**a cognitive induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge and understanding.**"
Source (quoting the paper): https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/how-to-stimulate-curiosity/
Paper: https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/PsychofCuriosity.pdf

The operative corollary for card text: **curiosity is strongest when the gap is small and specific**
— the reader must be made to feel they *almost* know. A card that withholds everything creates no gap
(nothing to be curious about); a card that explains everything closes it.

**Peter Mawhorter et al., "Towards a Theory of Choice Poetics"** — the framing/options/outcomes
anatomy, and the "blind choice" failure mode; summarised with quotes in §2f above.
Source: https://emshort.blog/2019/04/09/choice-poetics-peter-mawhorter/

# RULES THAT MULTIPLE PRACTITIONERS INDEPENDENTLY STATE

Ordered roughly by how many independent sources converge and how concrete the rule is.

### 1. Hard word budgets, and they are far smaller than writers expect
- **Failbetter (FL Writer Guidelines III):** root ≤30 words, branch ≤20 words, result ≤100 words.
- **Failbetter (Writing for Story Games):** "a single sentence for root and branch descriptions and a paragraph for event text"; ceiling of "four or five paragraphs."
- **inkle / Ingold (Haywire):** three-to-four paragraphs before a choice "caused a significant tail-off in people's interest in reading"; *Sorcery!* used "ruthlessly short chunks."
- **inkle / Ingold (Narrative News 2026):** "The number of lines is hugely important to me… have I earned the player's attention in this paragraph?"
- **CCG flavour text (Wonders):** "Each card has a limit, such as '100 characters'."
- **Emily Short (Prose Medium):** "each sentence would do the work of three or four sentences in the static prose version… closer to poetry than to conventional prose."
- **Yasmeen Khan (Points of Light I):** "We aim for exactly short enough" — with the explicit note that *too short* is also a failure ("Very tiny amounts of text aren't rich enough").

**Convergent number:** the pre-commitment card lands at ~1–2 sentences; the post-resolution text at
~1 paragraph (Failbetter's 100 words is the only published hard cap for exactly this slot).

### 2. Put the load-bearing information first; the reader is skimming
- **Failbetter:** "Put the important stuff first. This is the cornerstone and first rule of journalistic writing… If you can't do that, put the important stuff in the title."
- **inkle / Ingold:** "Text is something we engage with primarily on a visual level first. You see a wall of text, and your brain says that's going to take me about this long to read."
- **Emily Short:** the opening must have "confidence" — "It knows what it is going to be about, it sets its tone, and it directs the reader's attention to something interesting. It does not dither."
- **GDC MMO panel:** "Offering story in broad strokes may not feel as nuanced, but it will be far better understood."

### 3. The reader must be able to estimate the outcome *before* committing — "blind choice" is the canonical sin
- **Choice of Games (Slitt):** signal (a) potential story results, (b) which stats are tested, (c) relative difficulty — "especially important … when the potential consequences of an option are negative."
- **Choice of Games (judging rubric):** the baseball-cap rule — "'I put on a baseball cap' doesn't tell the player much, while 'I put on a baseball cap because I can pull it down to hide my face' indicate what kind of stat they may be raising."
- **Mawhorter, via Emily Short:** "blind choice (the infamous 'go left or go right' choice where the player has no framing information to establish the stakes)"; and *framing* is the under-examined third of choice anatomy.
- **Failbetter:** "A branch should almost always be a clear in-character action… make clear what the character will be doing if you click this option."
- **Emily Short (Prose Medium):** all text is a *Suggester* — "a willingness to foreground in description the functional aspects of an object."

### 4. Never state the difficulty of a check in the prose
- **Failbetter (Difficulty, Rewards and Punishment):** "don't talk about how hard the challenge is in the surrounding fiction… don't reference how hard a problem was, just that there was a success or a failure. If you want to emphasise how difficult something is, it's better to give it a cost."
- **Choice of Games (rubric):** outcome text that contradicts the player's stats ("no matter your swordfighting skill you lose the duel") is explicitly listed as unsatisfying.
- (Consistent with, not contradicted by, CoG/Slitt: signal *relative* difficulty **in the framing before the roll**; do not describe *absolute* difficulty **in the outcome after it**.)

### 5. One vivid particular beats an inventory of details — and the reader fills the rest
- **Yasmeen Khan / Failbetter:** the cognac glass — "the rest of the room, the warmth, the furniture, the shadow, is already there, wrapped in that one detail"; "the player carries the burden of shaping what's in the shadows that fill 90% of the canvas."
- **Failbetter (Parsimony):** "fires in the desert" — "let players fill in the blanks between your stories… The player's imagination is your ally."
- **Emily Short:** "not with a large number of common details… but with a small number of very particular ones."
- **CCG flavour guide:** "Leave something to the imagination. Just describe a small part of what's happening and let the reader fill in the rest."
- **Alexis Kennedy:** the enigmatic line as "fragments of a larger story… trails of breadcrumbs."

### 6. Never write the player's interiority; write only what is observable
- **Failbetter (Guidelines III):** "Be wary of putting words in the player's mouth, thoughts in their head, or feelings in their heart… Don't tell them they're scared; scare them."
- **Failbetter (Agency and Choices):** "stay away from telling a player that their character is surprised or elated or scared. It's the player's conception of their character that's important, rather than yours."
- **Yasmeen Khan:** "We're telling the player what's happening, but we shouldn't be telling them how to feel about it."
- **CCG flavour guide:** "Describe the results of what's happening, not what's happening itself" (bad: *He seethed with contempt*; good: *His lips curled and fists clenched*).
- **Choice of Games (rubric):** "if a player feels like it assigns qualities or actions to them that don't fit, it detracts from any feeling of satisfaction."

### 7. Failure text should be a *cost that sets up the next beat*, not a dead end
- **inkle / Ingold:** "get through at a cost, with that cost setting up the next beat of the story"; "keep the player failing-but-not-failed"; "fail forwards"; "squeaking through."
- **Failbetter:** "Punish characters — they'll love you for it… they'll remember being thrown into the weasel pits"; punishment should arrive as *a new manageable menace quality*, not as erased progress.
- **Choice of Games:** "endings where a player simply fails everything should not appear"; there should be "something dramatic and satisfying about every ending, even if the ending is unhappy."
- **Emily Short (on Mawhorter):** the "dead-end option" is a named defect.

### 8. Success text is allowed to be richer than failure text — text is the reward
- **Failbetter:** "get players used to the idea that text is a reward for play. So, you might want to make success text a bit longer or more interesting than failure text."
- **Alexis Kennedy:** "each piece of text was… a tiny reward in itself."
- (This is the *only* place the two sources sanction an asymmetric budget; everywhere else both push shorter.)

### 9. Handle names by tier: generic epithets for repeatable/anonymous roles, proper names only for fixed individuals
- **Yasmeen Khan / Failbetter (Points of Light II):** the explicit scale — *A Swivel-Eyed Patriot* (one of many) → *the Implacable Detective* (unique but facilitative) → *Mrs Plenty, Sinning Jenny, Dr Schlomo* (definite individuals). "Characters without individual names are more universal, less specific, less prone to continuity problems."
- **Failbetter (Pacing, on repeatable content):** "stealing a diamond or meeting a wolf, but **not** stealing the world's biggest diamond or meeting Lupius, father of all wolves."
- **CCG flavour guide:** "Quotes are good **if it's clear who's saying them and what the context is**" — an unattributed proper noun is a defect.
- **MtG (Rosewater):** consistent character voice required a *dedicated writer per named character* — i.e. naming a character is a recurring authorial commitment, not a one-line decoration.

### 10. Three options is the sweet spot, and none of them may be "do nothing"
- **inkle / Ingold:** "Two is too binary as if there's a correct choice, and four lacks focus" → Accept / Reject / Deflect.
- **Choice of Games:** "three or more options, none of which should be 'do nothing' options."
- **Failbetter (Agency and Choices)** dissents partially in favour of *sequences of small choices* over one wide menu — "five two-option choices rather than… ten options" — but agrees the wide always-available menu is the failure mode.

### 11. Simple syntax, plain verbs; flavour comes from nouns, not from sentence structure
- **Failbetter (Writing for Story Games):** "Use short, simple constructions. Don't write with a lot of semi-colons or parentheses or use endless clauses strung together with commas."
- **Yasmeen Khan:** "Says rather than utters. Shouts, not vociferises." + period flavour delivered by concrete props ("hansom cabs, cruet sets, tallboys").
- **Failbetter (Guidelines III):** "Be judicious with Victorian style… Treat it as seasoning, not an ingredient."
- **Choice of Games:** read aloud; hunt down "realize", "suddenly", "and then", repeated words, ambiguous antecedents.

### 12. Cut any sentence that does not do dramatic work
- **Choice of Games (Mary Duffy, via Vonnegut):** "Does this sentence reveal character or move the plot forward? If it doesn't, cut it."
- **Emily Short:** "if we have no such purpose in mind, and the details have no bearing on game play, they are probably best omitted. This is a medium that rewards restraint."
- **inkle / Ingold (Sparkling Dialogue):** the AC:Odyssey scene "doesn't go anywhere… It's not doing any *dramatic work*."
- **GDC MMO panel:** "Why not just skip to the good part with a few well-written quips?"

### 13. Repetition of information the reader already has is the most-named failure mode
- **inkle / Ingold:** the Duris/Sami repetition; and the in-world lore lecture ("Why is Rachel explaining what blade runners are? Everyone in this fictional world already knows").
- **Failbetter:** "All across London…" as a tell-don't-show tic; and clichés as sentences "swallowed without chewing."
- **Choice of Games:** repeated words / repeated question-types; Jason: "The thing that I want is to not be asked the same question repeatedly."
- **ink (Ingold):** the language ships syntax specifically so the chosen option's text and the resulting prose need **not** be identical.

### 14. A resolution must be legibly caused by what the player did
- **Choice of Games:** "The player should always understand why they reached the ending that they did. Having an ending come from nowhere… leaves a lasting bad impression."
- **Failbetter (Designing for FL):** show consequence *from a character* to make them tangible — "but a string of quality changes in a result makes each one less important."
- **inkle / Ingold:** "we expect there will be *some consequence* to the drama, and we don't yet know what it'll be."
- **Alexis Kennedy:** the text "provided clues towards the underlying system of the game."

### 15. Withhold enough to open a specific gap — but not so much that there is no gap
- **Loewenstein (1994):** curiosity is "the feeling of deprivation" produced by attention focusing on **a gap in one's knowledge**; the gap must be perceptible.
- **Alexis Kennedy:** "long enough to engage attention, but not long enough to feel like homework."
- **CCG flavour guide:** the final-clause twist — "*'One sting is enough to kill a person. And the undertaker if he doesn't use gloves.'*"
- **Yasmeen Khan:** "…they *say* few, if any, have survived the Pale Wastes" — the deliberate hedge as gap-opener.
- **Choice of Games:** "Games with a gripping premise make readers curious (**without confusing them!**)."
- **Rosewater:** the Rule of Three cadence, ending on the beat that turns — "*Now* is the time. *We* are the people. *This* is our action. Charge!"
