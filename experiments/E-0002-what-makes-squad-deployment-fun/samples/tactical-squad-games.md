# What makes sending the squad out fun: squad games with real tactical deployment

E-0002 research sample, track 2 of 3 ("squad games where deployment IS real gameplay"). Compiled 2026-10-20 by an Opus research agent plus three Opus sub-researchers, all with web search.

**Question.** In squad games where deployment is real gameplay, what exactly makes sending the squad out fun, and which of those fun sources depend on the tactical layer versus which could survive in a much simpler resolution (e.g. a sequence of visible dice rolls)?

**Games covered.** XCOM / XCOM 2, Battle Brothers, Darkest Dungeon, RimWorld, Wildermyth, Into the Breach, Jagged Alliance 2 / 3, Kenshi, Mordheim: City of the Damned, Frostpunk (scouts), Massive Chalice, Phoenix Point. Plus cross-cutting notes on auto-resolve and on how players perceive odds (Sid Meier, Gollop).

**How to read the citations.** Quotes come from pages a researcher actually fetched, and the fetch tool returns extracted text, so treat wording as near-verbatim and spot-check before quoting externally. **[snippet only]** means the text was seen only in a search result (page blocked or not opened). **[secondhand]** means another outlet reports the quote. GDC talks: none of the recordings were reachable. Only session pages and press coverage were, so nothing here quotes a GDC talk directly.

---

## TL;DR

- **Most of the fun players retell is not tactical.** Across the 13 games, the stories players share are about *who* went, *what happened to them*, and *what it cost*: the maimed rescue (RimWorld, Kenshi), the veteran gutted by a 5% swing (Battle Brothers, XCOM), the hero who broke under stress (Darkest Dungeon), the scouts sent "despite the warnings" (Frostpunk). Several designers (Sylvester, the Austins, Bourassa) and many critics call their own combat layer crude, thin or secondary.
- **What tactics actually buy** is (1) a spatial puzzle with mastery (Into the Breach, XCOM flanking), and (2) *ownership* of the outcome. Solomon ties permadeath to control: deaths must feel like "a risk I knew I was taking." Into the Breach: "every death felt like your own fault."
- **A dice resolution keeps nearly everything else** *if* it supplies ownership another way: choices before and between rolls (who, how deep, retreat or push, spend or save), visible honest odds, consequences that stick to named people, and failure that branches instead of ending.
- **Resolution with zero player input is the known failure mode.** Darkest Dungeon's designers patched out a risk-free safe play because "all the tension was lost". Frostpunk's scouts are called samey and riskless when odds are hidden and the scouts are anonymous.

## 1. XCOM: Enemy Unknown / XCOM 2 (Firaxis, 2012/2016)

**Shape of a deployment.** Strategy layer picks the mission (often one of several, losing the others), the squad (4-6 of a larger roster; wounded/tired soldiers benched), and loadout. Tactical layer: cover, overwatch, visible hit %, permadeath. After: promotions, wounds, deaths on a memorial wall, panic/country loss.

**What designers say the fun is**
- Permadeath needs perceived fairness, which is why control matters. Solomon: "this game _had_ to be turn-based because soldiers die in it" and "If I'm gonna make it really hard, and have these real consequences like permadeath ... then the player has to perceive the experience as completely fair." Death should read as "a risk I knew I was taking and it didn't pay off." Goal: players "always hanging on by their fingernails." ([Kill Screen, 2012](https://www.killscreen.com/we-talked-man-who-remaking-xcom-finally/))
- Attachment is earned through performance, not authored: "Maybe he's dead the next mission, or maybe he took out three guys and you're like, 'I like this guy…'" (same source). Nicknames unlock at rank; a memorial lists the dead.
- Story is the player's: Solomon recalled that the original's tiny pixel soldiers led players to invent backstories, and said the team "build the stage and give the player props to tell the story" rather than forcing one ([Kotaku, via search snippet; page 403 to fetch](https://kotaku.com/remembering-the-fallen-and-the-decisions-for-which-the-5916627)).
- Randomness is wanted, but bounded by emotion, not math. Solomon (XCOM 2): "If you shave off the lows, a lot of the time you're shaving off the highs." / "Players may view that number not from a mathematical sense, but from an emotional sense." / "That 85 percent isn't actually 85 percent." / "We don't want the players missing multiple 85 percent shots, because then the game starts to feel punitive." ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/jake-solomon-explains-the-careful-use-of-randomness-in-i-xcom-2-i-))
- EU already had a hidden streak breaker. Lead designer Ananda Gupta: "On Normal and Easy modes, there's a bad streak breaker ... the fewer soldiers you have the stronger this effect becomes" ([GameBanshee citing PCGamesN](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/112504-xcom-enemy-within-adds-qsave-scumq-option.html)).
- War of the Chosen doubled down on soldier-as-story: bonds, negative traits from bad missions, photobooth posters. Lead writer Scott Wittbecker (search snippet of xcom.com interview, now redirected): bonds and photobooth are "ways the gameplay promotes the player's personal story", and connection features are "left somewhat vague on purpose" ([xcom.com, via search](https://xcom.com/news/the-story-behind-the-story-of-xcom-2-war-of-the-chosen/amp)). GameSpot: the posters change nothing mechanically but are "a tacit acknowledgement that your team and their connections matter" ([GameSpot review](https://gamespot.com/reviews/xcom-2-war-of-the-chosen-review/1900-6416748/)).
- Solomon later found randomness wrong for *heroes*: in Midnight Suns "as we kept putting randomness in the abilities of heroes, it felt terrible" ([GoNintendo via GameSpot](https://gonintendo.com/contents/4719-marvel-s-midnight-suns-devs-say-their-attempt-to-add-in-xcom-elements-felt-terrible)). Random misses suit expendable rookies, not named stars the player didn't grow.

**What players/critics say**
- "That's XCOM, baby": the meme for 95% misses and 5% hits, said "not with admiration" ([TIME Techland](https://techland.time.com/?p=147750); [GameBanshee, Gollop Chamber](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k4xa3)). A 2012 Steam commenter: "I've had guys hit 20% chances, and guys miss 90% chances. That's Xcom baby!" [snippet only; thread URL not pinned]. Fans even built a "Fair RNG" mod though its author grants "the RNG in XCOM is already fair" ([Nexus](https://www.nexusmods.com/xcom2/mods/482?tab=docs)).
- Game Informer staff: customization + permadeath make each mission "a continual barrage of nerve-racking, life-or-death experiences"; one editor keeps random names and grants "nicknames based on their performance on the battlefield"; "Sharing these experiences is part of the fun." ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2012/10/11/share-your-xcom-war-stories.aspx))
- Game Informer editorial on permadeath: "By using permanent death, games impart something unique: guilt." / "Their absence affects you emotionally because you controlled them, and not merely interacted with them." ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2012/10/16/gone-forever-how-and-why-permadeath-affects-us))
- Layers: "The strategy layer presents the game's only losing condition ... the tactical layer provides the game's only win condition"; "the best choice for winning may be the worst choice for not losing" (Harold Myles, [Game Developer blog](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/xcom-clear-choices-tough-decisions)).

**Related theory (Sid Meier, GDC 2010 keynote).** Players at 3:1 odds expect to win and call losses cheating: "Player psychology has nothing to do with rational thought." / "When random things happen, paranoia strikes the heart of the gamer." ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/2010/03/12/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech)). Gollop: "human beings are not very good at evaluating probabilities" ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k4xa3)).

**Analysis.** XCOM's deployment fun has four layers: (1) choosing *who goes* under scarcity (wounds, fatigue, mission choice that forfeits others); (2) per-shot tension from *visible percentages* attached to a named soldier; (3) *irreversible* consequences (death, wounds, traits, memorial); (4) attachment that grows from a soldier's own record (kills, clutch shots, nicknames, bonds). Only the in-mission positioning puzzle (flank, cover, overwatch) truly needs tactics. Its job for the other three is *fairness/ownership*: Solomon ties permadeath to control so deaths feel like the player's risk. A dice-roll system keeps (1), (2), (3) and (4) but must find another way to make the player own each loss — the risk must have been chosen.

## 2. Battle Brothers (Overhype Studios, 2017)

**Shape.** World-map mercenary company. Take contracts, hire from a lottery of backgrounds (farmer, sellsword, cripple…) with partial info, pay daily wages. Hex tactics with visible hit chances capped at 95%. Permadeath; injuries and traits change over time; world-map events key off who is in the company.

**Designers**
- "If a Battle Brother is killed in combat he is dead forever, his experience and skills will be lost." (2014 feature list, [indierpgs](https://indierpgs.com/2014/02/battle-brothers-announced/))
- Event system: companions "sometimes have their own agenda, they have strengths and weaknesses in character, they may change with time." (Dev Blog #43, [moddb](https://www.moddb.com/news/dev-blog-43-introducing-the-event-system)) [snippet only]
- Developer Casey: "Many events were designed to proc based on who is in the party (and other triggers)… Events that require player input and carry rewards/pitfalls are obviously better" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/135511757698190329))
- Traits change with experience: a brother traumatised by orcs may fear greenskins, and "as he grows in experience and is hardened by battle, he will eventually overcome his fear and lose the trait again." (Dev Blog #111, [GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/k4hc8))

**Critics / players**
- PC Gamer: "the veteran soldier suddenly gutted, lost forever behind the veil of permadeath" and "I just have to make enough to fix my gear, hire a new sword, and go on to my next contract." RPGFan: "an incredible group of fighters who I got to know by name." Cultured Vultures: "a platform for vast quantities of self-made stories, both cheerful and devastating." ([GameBanshee roundup](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k648d))
- Player mutait: "fretting over my old favorites, not wanting to get too connected to new guys until they'd survived a few battles." Player tgb123: "After I had a party wipe on the first or second mission for the 5th or 6th time, I just didn't care any more." ([Quarter to Three](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/battle-brothers-hits-early-access/76758))
- On the 95% cap: "the roll is 96, he misses. Next turn, the fast beast then counter and hit with a 12% accuracy and kill someone." Reply: "100% hits make you complacent and it'd rob you of occasional 'oh crap!' moments." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/2/1743353798881926567))
- Players on events: they "add a living layer on top of your company and travels"; one asks for a history tab to remember "when Jost the Eunuch became a brothel legend". Others: events "occur too infrequently", "too shallow and meaningless." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/135511757698190329))
- Auto-resolve: "I hate the idea of an auto-win but I hate even more the idea of a campaign wipe"; "I honestly would like an autobattle feature (as opposed to autoskip)" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/135510194247672603))

**Analysis.** *Before* is rich and non-tactical: contract choice (risk tier vs pay), the hiring lottery, the payroll squeeze. *After* is non-tactical too: injury/death, trait gain and loss, repair bills. *Between*, trait-keyed events produce the anecdotes ("Jost the Eunuch"). The hex layer supplies the second-to-second "will he survive this swing" tension, and visible rolls could carry that if each roll is pinned to one named brother at a clear stake. Warnings: repeated wipes kill attachment, and players only want auto-resolve for fights already decided. The fun is in the *close* fights.

## 3. Darkest Dungeon (Red Hook Studios, 2016)

**Shape.** Hamlet (recruit, heal stress, cure quirks) → expedition with bought provisions and torch → 4-slot rank combat. Stress builds; at 100 a resolve check yields a Virtue (rare buff) or an Affliction (debuff that spreads stress). Quirks accrue permanently. Retreat is always possible at a cost. Permadeath.

**Designers**
- GDC 2016 "Darkest Dungeon: A Design Postmortem" (Tyler Sigman) covers layering the Affliction/stress system onto the game's other inspirations ([GDC Vault session page](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023089/contactUs)). Coverage says Sigman traced Affliction to "human experience" and Buck Compton in *Band of Brothers* [secondhand] ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-darkest-dungeon-got-inspiration-from-band-of-brothers-and-aliens)).
- Bourassa: "The idea was to create that sense of being a manager or a sports coach… when they're really stressed out sometimes they'll ignore you or do something on their own. It's frustrating to you as a player, but that's the point." Sigman: "We all have good parts and bad parts, and we all react to stress differently… And we wanted to map that onto fantasy heroes." ([Kill Screen](https://killscreen.com/darkest-dungeon-explores-psychological-horror-dungeon-crawl))
- Bourassa: "I think the game is at its best when you feel like you're playing with a high level of risk and reward." On players stun-locking the last enemy to heal and reset stress: "All the tension was lost." Sigman: "the flaws are key to the characters" ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/darkest-dungeon-designers-really-arent-sadists-were-realists/))
- Bourassa: "Afflicted characters are like loaded guns, you never know when they'll go off!" / heroes "develop a variety of long-lasting 'quirks' as a result of their experiences" / "death is permanent, so your decisions really do matter." ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2014/12/06/old-school-rpg-darkest-dungeon-unveiled-ps4-ps-vita/))
- Red Hook: "We want to create a game about flawed, human heroes not immutable killing machines." ([GameBanshee/RPGWatch](http://www.gamebanshee.com/kp83y)). Bourassa: "RPGs are more interesting when things go wrong." [secondhand] ([GoNintendo via Eurogamer](https://gonintendo.com/contents/20902-darkest-dungeon-dev-says-the-game-s-difficulty-was-inspired-by-tabletop-rpgs-and-the))

**Players**
- "Think of it like Cthulhu poker… The thing is, the game lets you fold your hand and cut your losses." ([GOG review](https://www.gog.com/u/Witzelsucht/reviews?page=2))
- "Many were named either RS## (redshirt, intended to die or be discarded later)… I was happy sending countless redshirts to their doom, but not my beloved officers!" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/262060/discussions/0/392184522723392076))
- "I would rather retreat, than loose another one." / "losing Reynauld feels like the biggest gut punch to me." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/262060/discussions/0/1457328392104883579))

**Analysis.** DD is the clearest case that deployment fun can live outside tactics. Its signature beats are already chance rolls with visible stakes: the 100-stress resolve check (Virtue/Affliction), quirk acquisition, crits and deaths' door. The expedition is a push-your-luck structure ("Cthulhu poker": go deeper or fold). The combat layer is thin (4 ranks, skill-by-position). What must survive is *agency during the run*: the designers patched the risk-free safe play because tension died without a real choice. Players invent the redshirt/officer split themselves, so attachment is earned by survival.

## 4. RimWorld (Ludeon Studios, Tynan Sylvester, 2018)

**Shape.** Colony sim. An AI storyteller (Cassandra, Phoebe, Randy) paces incidents. Colonists have traits, backstories, relationships, body-part health. Combat is crude real-time with pause. Caravans travel the world map and get ambushed.

**Designer**
- "In this way, the simulation is a co-author of stories with the player." / "We only need to show the simulation equivalent of moving balls and let the player layer in their own emotional perceptions." / "Choose the minimum representation that supports the kinds of stories you want to generate." / "In general, lean on the simple side." (*The Simulation Dream*, 2013, [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/the-simulation-dream))
- "The real engine of story is the gameplay systems themselves." / "The game tries to hurt but not kill the colony." / "Raiders, when they win a battle, will steal or kidnap and leave, instead of killing everyone." / "The storyteller eases off when the colony population drops." ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-i-rimworld-i-fleshes-out-the-i-dwarf-fortress-i-formula))
- "I'd rather make the best small-scale story generator I can… Not into becoming a mediocre story generator sewn onto a mediocre version of Civilization." / "The experiences and feelings and stories of a handful of individuals in a tough situation." ([Cultured Vultures](https://culturedvultures.com/tynan-sylvester-rimworld/))
- GDC 2017 session framed RimWorld "as a story generator rather than a game" ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024232/-RimWorld-Contrarian-Ridiculous-and)).
- From his book, as cited: "But the human mind sees stories anyway, given the slightest of suggestions." ([habrador blog](https://blog.habrador.com/2019/06/why-rimworld-sold-more-than-1-million.html))

**Critics / players**
- PCGamesN: "The combat system was originally designed for a Jagged Alliance style game, and perhaps is the most awkward of the lot." Yet "Social and combat logs make discussions and fights so much more descriptive… it's possible to get seriously invested in the lives of your colonists" and "RimWorld's story systems remember when big events have happened and who was involved." ([PCGamesN review](https://www.pcgamesn.com/rimworld/review))
- Player: "one colonist had an arm chopped off by a scyther, and another had a leg blown off by a centipede… He had 1 hour to live when I got him to the hospital… Rimworld likes to pile crises on top of crises." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/294100/discussions/0/2280456883444343113))

**Analysis.** RimWorld is the game the designer named, and its own designer says its combat is the least important system. The retold stories hinge on who got hurt and what it set off, not on clever flanking. Its levers are all non-tactical: storyteller pacing that stacks crises, failure that "hurts but not kills" (kidnap, maim, steal), body-part injuries that persist, and logs that remember who did what. What needs real-time control: the "got him to the hospital with an hour left" intervention. A dice version can approximate it with a decision between rolls ("risk a rescue?").

## 5. Wildermyth (Worldwalker Games, Nate & Anne Austin, 2021)

**Shape.** Procedural campaign told as comic panels. Heroes start as farmers, age, fall in love, feud, get transformed (flame arm, wolf head), retire, return as legacy heroes. Grid tactics between story vignettes. At 0 HP the **Mortal Choice**: pick a lasting wound or a heroic death.

**Designers**
- "combat is a huge pillar, but that core fantasy of building this roster of legends is the heart of the game, to us." / "The focus here is on character development, NOT on plot." / "The sacrifices the heroes make and the way their bodies change over time is a huge part of the story we're telling." / "Personalities affect which heroes will naturally fall in love, and which will hate each other." / legacy heroes exist "so you don't have to be afraid of getting attached to them." ([Turn Based Lovers](https://turnbasedlovers.com/10-turns-interview/with-wildermyth-developer))
- On XCOM: "we love the stories that are generated. But we wanted a lot more detail about the soldiers." / "they start off as farmers but like somewhere along the line something happens… they're transformed in these really visual ways." ([GameBanshee via RPS](https://www.gamebanshee.com/xykr3))
- GDC IGS abstract: "What makes a player get attached to a character? Particularly a generated one?" Pitfalls: "breach of trust, acting out of character, or rage quits." ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027614/Independent-Games-Summit-Session-Getting))
- Mortal Choice options include "Hero is maimed (loses a random human limb)", "Hero loses an eye", "Hero dies, deals 10 damage", "Hero dies, +3 armor to party" ([wiki](https://wildermyth.com/wiki/Mortal_Choice)).

**Critics / players**
- RPG Site: "The combat in Wildermyth is not very deep… However, this combat is not really the main draw." But also: "I also started seeing my 'characters' as a box-of-attributes fed into an algorithm." ([RPG Site](https://www.rpgsite.net/review/12107-wildermyth-review))
- Game Informer: "While the combat is solid, these frequent story vignettes are absolutely the highlight." ([GI](https://gameinformer.com/review/wildermyth/wildermyth-review-tales-and-tactics))
- Eurogamer (via roundup): "they have changed. They're older. They've known love and loss… lose some in battle – this itself is always a choice, rather beautifully." ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/af4xc))
- Destructoid: "The story feeds into the battles, and those battles feed back into the story." ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/wildermyth-impressions-procedural-story-rpg/))

**Analysis.** Designers and critics alike place the heart in the roster, not the battle. The Mortal Choice is the key bridge: a combat result (0 HP) becomes a *player-chosen*, permanent mark. That converts directly to dice: a roll drops a hero, and the player picks "lose an arm" or "die well." Aging, transformations, relationships and legacy all hang off time and events, not the grid. Tactics carry build expression and replay depth for min-maxers. The "box of attributes" complaint is the warning: once vignettes repeat, attachment collapses whatever the resolution model.

## 6. Into the Breach (Subset Games, 2018)

**Shape.** Three mechs, small grid, enemy attacks telegraphed a turn ahead, nearly no randomness. Pilots gain XP. When a timeline is lost, one pilot carries over to the next.

**Designer (Justin Ma)**
- "We prefer games with clear rulesets, and were interested in pursuing something with less randomness than FTL." / "We wanted to make something where every death felt like your own fault." / "it's very clear how or why you failed so that you can feel like you can improve." / "we hope the limited reliance on random chance in Into the Breach helps make the player feel in control." ([Game Developer, Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-subset-games-i-into-the-breach-i-))
- On the one random mechanic they kept (building resist chance) and XCOM: "as much as players are bothered by failing a 95% chance shot in XCOM" / "most take no issue with a 5% chance effect where the result is in their favor." Without it, "if you knew you were going to lose the game from the next enemy attack, there was no reason to hit End Turn." / "a very unsatisfying way for games to end – with a whimper rather than a bang." ([Cliqist](https://cliqist.com/2018/03/06/into-the-breach-building-a-better-mech-with-subset-games-justin-ma/))

**Critics**
- "You get to pick one pilot to keep when you switch to a new timeline" / "your mission is not to win but to keep your VIP alive so you can come back stronger next time" ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/into-the-breach/permadeath))
- Pilot backstories exist but "you don't have to read it to understand the game" ([Cloudfall Studios](https://www.cloudfallstudios.com/blog/2018/3/3/what-i-learned-into-the-breach-and-narrative-through-dialogue))

**Analysis.** The control case. Its fun is the perfect-information puzzle, and it *does not survive* conversion to dice. Attachment is deliberately thin. Two transferable lessons: (1) chance is accepted when it rescues and resented when it betrays, so luck should lean toward saving the player; (2) a loss must be legible, with a visible cause, so the player can own it.

## 7. Jagged Alliance 2 (Sir-Tech, 1999) and Jagged Alliance 3 (Haemimont, 2023)

**Shape.** Hire voiced mercs from the in-game AIM website on timed contracts. Mercs have biographies, likes/dislikes, grudges; some refuse to work with others or quit. Turn-based AP tactics. Strategic map of sectors to take.

**Designers**
- Ian Currie (JA designer): "we leaned on ethnicities, attitudes, interesting backgrounds, accents and of course good voice acting… The more they come across like real individuals, the more attached the player is to them and that has multiple benefits." ([GameBanshee/GameStar](http://www.gamebanshee.com/9wyy8))
- Haemimont (JA3 Dev Diary #1): "Jagged Alliance was always about the mercs!… they interject in conversations, form relations, make jokes, hold grudges… each merc is a potential main character, so all of them should be worthy of this role!" / "since most of your mercs are operating on a contract, time is money!" / content changes "depending on which mercs are in the current group." ([GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/9kydr))
- JA3 hid hit chance on purpose, "to try and avoid players treating the game like XCOM" ([Wargamer preview](https://www.wargamer.com/jagged-alliance-3/preview)). Their optional mod's note: "Visible chance to hit is not the kind of core experience we had in mind" [secondhand, quoted by a player] ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1084160/discussions/0/4294818447588353951)).

**Critics / players**
- "Mercs who really hate one another will refuse to re-up their contract with you or even quit the team mid-assignment." ([coin-return LP](https://forums.coin-return.org/threads/lets-play-jagged-alliance-2.704))
- "occasionally, some will die in action before you get the chance to hire them" / experience "motivates them to ask for more money the next time their contract comes up for renewal" ([JustGamesRetro](https://www.justgamesretro.com/?p=8379))
- GamesFirst (1999): "Some of your mercs will hold grudges against others, and the dialogue between them is quite humorous." / "the mediocre mercenaries you hire early on will improve through experience and training" ([GamesFirst](https://gamesfirst.com/articles/1999/jagged-alliance-2-2/))
- JA3, "some may even refuse to join you or even renegotiate their cost if you hire someone they dislike." ([Impulse Gamer](https://impulsegamer.com/jagged-alliance-3-pc-review-command-contract-conquer))
- Hidden odds split players: "clicking the highest % numbers on each target seems like the opposite of tactical to me" vs "Other games display the chance for a reason, too." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1084160/discussions/0/4294818447588353951))
- Banter split critics: EIP calls it "meaningless" and suggests muting it ([EIP](https://eip.gg/reviews/jagged-alliance-3/)); Wargamer: "corny one liner after corny one liner", while the management layer "adds a needed layer of preparation and planning." ([Wargamer](https://www.wargamer.com/jagged-alliance-3/preview))

**Analysis.** JA's signature deployment fun is people-management *before* the fight: shopping for mercs, chemistry constraints (who refuses whom), the contract clock, rising prices as mercs improve. All of it survives with dice. JA3 is a direct data point on odds: hiding them to stop "safe-shot" play backfired with many players. Banter lands when it is situational and tied to a specific pair; generic chatter gets muted. Critics also note JA personalities are *fixed*: attachment comes from voice and friction, not arcs.

## 8. Kenshi (Lo-Fi Games, Chris Hunt, 2018)

**Shape.** Open-world squad sandbox. Combat is mostly auto-attack with positioning. Defeat usually means knocked out, robbed, enslaved, or limbs lost, rarely death. Skills train by use, toughness by being beaten.

**Designer**
- "The real stories of the game are emergent, the players of Kenshi can tell you better stories than the plots of any linear games out there." / "Kenshi is unique in that whatever happens to your characters it rarely means death and gives you a chance to try and save them." ([game-guide.fr interview](https://game-guide.fr/243268-kenshi-interview-de-chris-hunt-pour-la-sortie-du-rpg-sword-punk-6-12/))
- "if a character's legs get crippled, they'll have to fight from crawling on the ground." / "if you leave a wound unbandaged, you can still bleed to death even after surviving a fight." ([Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/?p=504862))
- Headline quote: "Too many games let their players succeed… that's mind-numbingly boring to me." ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/survival-crafting/too-many-games-let-their-players-succeed-says-developer-of-notoriously-tough-survival-rpg-kenshi-and-thats-mind-numbingly-boring-to-me/))

**Critics / players**
- The Register: "There are no 'abilities' or 'spells' to utilise beyond micromanaging unit positions… yet stories happen all the time." ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2019/05/31/kenshi_sandblasted_swordpunk_where_the_dungeon_master_wants_everyone_dead/))
- Tampere Playlab: "you direct your character(s) by mouse clicks to auto attack." / "Failure is an option, and some endings can be quite beautiful." ([Playlab](https://blogs.tuni.fi/playlab/game-reviews/lunar-sims-with-slavery-and-misogyny-a-review-of-kenshi/))
- Player: "Beep has lost a leg. So I carried him through the fog… I couldn't leave him behind now! Both or no one would make it." Replies: "no way I reload or savescum, this was so beautiful" / "did you buy him a prosthetic leg? you owe him one." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/233860/discussions/0/3342162929518975269))

**Analysis.** The strongest evidence that the fun survives with almost no tactical control: auto-combat, yet "stories happen all the time." The engine is **failure that branches**. Defeat creates a new goal (rescue, escape, prosthetic) instead of a game over. Tension lives in the player's choices between moments: go back for Beep, carry him, pay for the leg. A roll chain with a "rescue or abandon?" decision carries most of this.

## 9. Mordheim: City of the Damned (Rogue Factor, 2015)

**Shape.** Warhammer warband skirmish. Out-of-action warriors take a post-battle injury roll (death, lost limb, phobia, recovery). Upkeep, treatment costs, a benefactor's deadlines.

**Designer / critics / players**
- Lead designer Bruno Parenteau: "if your character dies in battle, he's gone… So he might not die, but he could end of with one arm chopped off… It will affect the development of your warband." ([GameWatcher](https://www.gamewatcher.com/2014-14-04-mordheim-city-of-the-damned-all-about-hardcore-warhammer-factions-revealed))
- TheSixthAxis: "random permanent injuries for your troops, whether psychological disorders and missing limbs, or simply death… losing the same limb twice will retire them from the warband completely." But: "aside from mourning their accumulated experience or equipment, I very rarely felt this for my warband members… I even stopped naming them, which is a shame." Gameplay "revolves around a mixture of turn based strategy and sheer luck." ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2016/02/03/mordheim-city-of-the-damned-review/))
- Players: "losing a rank 10, fully skilled up veteran because the game randomly spawned a Rat Ogre"; "riskier deployments offer better items and wyrdstone"; "Mordheim is about dealing with loss." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/276810/discussions/1/208684375418638725))
- Players asked for the injury odds; another posted the tabletop 2D6 injury table ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/276810/discussions/1/490125737484268693)).

**Analysis.** The main consequence beat is *already* a visible chance roll, and players want its odds shown. Risk tier is chosen before deploying. The cautionary half: a reviewer stopped naming warriors because they had no personality. Harsh consequences without character produce *accounting* (lost XP, gear), not grief. And losses from unreadable causes (a random spawn) read as unfair.

## 10. Frostpunk (11 bit studios, 2018): scout expeditions

**Shape.** The "deployment without tactics" case inside a tactics-free city builder. Send a scout team along a node map; arrival triggers a text event with a choice. Some choices hide a chance of losing the team.

**Critics / players**
- "send scouts out to explore the larger world" / "a map with thumbtacks on it" / locations "offer some variety of reward" ([Caltrops](https://caltrops.com/?p=18525))
- "Sending out your search party can take days, but often bring hope to the people" / "bad news can also come your way when you find a dead city, or the frozen corpses of other travellers." ([Xbox Tavern](https://www.xboxtavern.com/frostpunk-review/))
- Scouting "provides no challenge or any kind of real danger" / "next to no kind of randomisation to the events you encounter" ([Checkpoint](https://checkpointgaming.net/reviews/2018/04/frost-punk-review-not-winter-wonderland/)) [snippet only]. Fandom review section titled "Scouts, Events, and Sameyness" ([Fandom](https://www.fandom.com/articles/frostpunk-review)) [snippet only]
- Players at Tesla City: "I sent in my scout team at Tesla City despite the warnings. I was saddened to learn they died" / "I know 5 people dying is rough, but I have 10 automatons now… Seems like a good trade to me." / "the lightning scared me away and I noped outta there." / "It's definitely random and I think it's weighted heavily to be safe instead of lethal" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1693795812298876657))
- "It's probably another RNG kill your scout party thing tbh" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/323190/discussions/0/1735464254636154780))

**Analysis.** Text-event deployment produces real feeling when the risk is *warned and chosen* and the cost/gain is visible ("good trade"). Its weaknesses are all fixable without tactics: hidden odds (players can't tell if a death was RNG), anonymous scouts ("5 people"), a fixed, repeating event pool, and routine trips without danger.

## 11. Massive Chalice (Double Fine, Brad Muir, 2015)

**Shape.** 300-year war. Heroes age and die of old age; bloodlines pass traits; the Keep marries heroes to breed the next generation. XCOM-like tactics.

**Designer**
- Brad Muir: "If you mess up, say you lose your high-level sniper, now you have a sniper-shaped hole in your squad that you have to fill." / "You have to get a rookie in there, and that puts stress on the other parts of your squad". Article paraphrase: forcing players to mix up squads was "the number one goal" of aging. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/multigenerational-mayhem-exploring-i-massive-chalice-i-s-bloodline-system))

**Critics** (via [OpenCritic](https://opencritic.com/game/326/-/reviews) and [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massive_Chalice))
- IGN: tactics "would be stronger without so many opportunities to lose due to bad luck." Destructoid: "all I got for my click click clicking was combat with bigger numbers on the same handful of stages" / bloodline payoff "is not worth the rote meat grinder to get there."
- PC Gamer: "the bloodlines concept was by far Massive Chalice's greatest feature"; combat "well-executed but insubstantial." TrueAchievements: "Decisions you make early on will impact the tide of war decades later."
- Shacknews: "There will be periods when an entire generation of heroes will suddenly perish." / "it's all worth it to see your Houses flourish." ([Shacknews](https://shacknews.com/article/89797/massive-chalice-review-my-cup-runneth-over))

**Analysis.** Critics put the fun in the strategic layer: the generational clock, long bets on lineages, holes in the squad. Tactics were "thin," "rote," "luck-heavy." Evidence that before/after consequences can carry a squad game while the during-mission layer stays weak, and a warning that long repetitive missions between those beats become a "meat grinder". Keep the stretch between consequences short.

## 12. Phoenix Point (Snapshot Games, Julian Gollop, 2019)

**Shape.** XCOM successor that replaced hit percentages with simulated ballistics (each bullet traced), free aim, and body-part damage.

**Designer (Gollop)**
- "Each bullet has a separate trajectory and damage whatever it hits." / "It's would be difficult to give the player meaningful data about expected outcomes from an attack." ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/news/119539-phoenix-point-realistic-ballistics-are-in.html))
- "Missed shots will definitely hit something, and potentially damage or destroy it." ([RPG Codex](https://rpgcodex.net/content.php?id=10618))
- "The fresh recruits you take on, you can treat them like cannon fodder." / "Your experienced soldiers will be very valuable. You don't want to lose them." ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/how-phoenix-point-takes-turn-based-tactics-where-the-genre-hasnt-gone/))
- On RNG generally: "I now understand that human beings are not very good at evaluating probabilities." On Chaos Reborn: "The tension of each battle was enhanced by the rapid turnarounds, plans thwarted, and opportunities grasped," though a "significant group of players loathed the RNG aspect". ([GameBanshee, Gollop Chamber](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k4xa3))

**Critics / players**
- "Taking your shots isn't a binary stab at an RNG percentage." / body-part hits cause effects "like bleeding" ([Worth Playing](https://worthplaying.com/article/2020/2/7/reviews/118007-pc-review-phoenix-point/))
- Player: "In Xcom, we were given a clear percentage. If that exists in PP, I'm not seeing it." Reply: "It is not a precise science, and that is by design." ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/839770/discussions/0/601893317626600700/))

**Analysis.** Ballistics trade a readable number for a *visible cause* and *graded outcomes* (a miss still hits something; a hit can cripple a specific limb). The simulation needs the tactical layer; the three ideas behind it do not: misses that still matter, graded/body-part outcomes that persist, and a shown cause for each failure. The cost, players losing the readable odds, is another vote for showing numbers.

## 13. Cross-cutting: auto-resolve and the perception of odds

- **Auto-resolve.** Total War players: "After 3 or 4 fights i just want to auto resolve my way to victory" vs "it does take away that satisfaction of winning a battle due to your own ability and effort" vs "I do manual battles, when auto-resolve says that i lose a unit which i don´t want to lose" ([Steam search results](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1142710/discussions/0/570417860285011076/)) [snippet only; individual threads not pinned]. Battle Brothers players want it only for already-won fights (above). **Pattern: players skip resolution when the outcome is obvious and slow down when a valued unit is at risk.**
- **Odds perception.** Sid Meier: players at 3:1 feel entitled to win; "Player psychology has nothing to do with rational thought." / "When random things happen, paranoia strikes the heart of the gamer." ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/2010/03/12/quotes-from-sid-meiers-keynote-gdc-speech)). Firaxis and Civ Revolution both quietly bent displayed odds toward expectations (see XCOM). Into the Breach's asymmetry: lucky saves are welcome, unlucky betrayals are resented.

## Principles, sorted

Tags: **(a)** needs tactical gameplay · **(b)** works with simple chance resolution · **(c)** unclear / depends on what replaces tactics.

### P1. Choosing who goes, under scarcity, is a core decision. (b)
XCOM's mission choice forfeits other missions and wounded/tired soldiers sit out. Battle Brothers sells contracts by risk tier with a payroll ticking. JA2 makes hiring a chemistry puzzle where mercs refuse each other. Massive Chalice forces churn ("a sniper-shaped hole"). Mordheim lets you pick riskier maps for better loot. None of this touches the tactical layer; it is the strategic layer feeding the mission. *Implication:* the roster must be bigger than the party, members must be unequal and partly unavailable (hurt, tired, feuding), and jobs must differ in risk and reward.

### P2. Visible odds on a named person make each roll felt. (b)
XCOM's emotional core is a percentage hanging over a soldier the player knows. Players demand to see numbers (JA3's hidden-odds backlash, Phoenix Point players missing "a clear percentage," Mordheim players asking for the injury table). Frostpunk's hidden scout odds made deaths read as "another RNG kill." A dice sequence can show exactly this, and arguably more clearly than tactics, because the roll *is* the content.

### P3. Tune luck for feeling, not math: let it rescue more than it betrays. (b)
Meier: "Player psychology has nothing to do with rational thought." Solomon: "That 85 percent isn't actually 85 percent" and missing several 85s "starts to feel punitive." XCOM EU shipped a hidden streak breaker that strengthens as the squad shrinks. Into the Breach: players hate failing a 95% but welcome a 5% save. Yet "If you shave off the lows, a lot of the time you're shaving off the highs," and players defend the 95% cap for its "oh crap!" moments. *Implication:* keep real upsets, bound streaks, bias saving throws toward the player, and be honest about what is shown.

### P4. The player must own the risk. Tactics is one way to get ownership, not the only way. (c)
Solomon: permadeath forced turn-based control so death reads as "a risk I knew I was taking." Ma: "every death felt like your own fault." In dice games ownership has to come from the choices around the roll: who was sent, which risk tier, whether to push on, whether to spend the potion. Frostpunk's best moment is a warned risk taken "despite the warnings." Whether pre-roll and between-roll choices give as much ownership as moving the soldier yourself is the open question. Midnight Suns ("randomness in the abilities of heroes... felt terrible") and the redshirt/officer split suggest randomness hurts most on characters the player can't protect. *Test for E-0002:* give the player a lever on every high-stakes roll and see whether losses feel owned.

### P5. Decision points during the run, especially push-your-luck, carry the tension. (b), provided they exist
Darkest Dungeon is "Cthulhu poker" where you can "fold your hand," and its designers patched out the risk-free option because "all the tension was lost." Kenshi's best stories are "go back for him?" choices; RimWorld's are rescues mid-crisis. These need a *choice*, not a *grid*. A roll chain with stop/continue, rescue/abandon and spend/save at each step keeps it. Zero-input resolution loses it: this is the gap E-0001 run 1 hit.

### P6. Consequences must stick to the person: graded, visible, permanent. (b)
Limbs (RimWorld, Kenshi, Mordheim, Phoenix Point), quirks and afflictions (DD), traits gained and lost (Battle Brothers, XCOM WotC), aging and transformation (Wildermyth, Massive Chalice). Graded outcomes beat hit/miss (Gollop: "Missed shots will definitely hit something"). Mordheim's post-battle injury table and DD's resolve check show the consequence beat working as a plain roll.

### P7. Failure branches the story instead of ending it. (b)
Sylvester: "The game tries to hurt but not kill the colony"; raiders "steal or kidnap and leave." Hunt: defeat "rarely means death and gives you a chance to try and save them." Wildermyth's Mortal Choice turns 0 HP into a chosen wound or heroic death. Kenshi's enslavement and prosthetic arcs are retold more than any victory. Counter-evidence: repeated wipes kill investment ("I just didn't care any more"). Losses should produce new goals.

### P8. Attachment is earned by survival record plus personality, and it needs memory. (b)
"Maybe he took out three guys and you're like, 'I like this guy…'" (Solomon). Players hold back "until they'd survived a few battles" (Battle Brothers) and split redshirts from officers (DD). Personality is the multiplier: without it the Mordheim reviewer "stopped naming them." Logs that "remember when big events have happened and who was involved" (RimWorld), nicknames by rank, memorial walls, photobooth posters, and a requested "history tab" ("when Jost the Eunuch became a brothel legend") are all non-tactical memory devices. One caveat: in XCOM some remembered deeds are *player-made* clutch plays. Dice-made deeds are the game's, not the player's, so P4 applies to attachment too.

### P9. Relationships and trait-triggered events make the squad a cast. (b)
JA2/JA3 likes, dislikes and grudges; XCOM WotC bonds (and bondmate grief); Wildermyth romances and feuds; Battle Brothers events that "proc based on who is in the party." These are strategic-layer systems that read the roster. Warnings: generic banter gets muted (JA3), events that are rare or shallow get called "meaningless" (Battle Brothers). Situational lines tied to a specific pair land.

### P10. Surprise needs a varied source; tactics get it from simulation, dice need a rich event pool. (c)
Tactical surprise comes from simulation: stray bullets (Phoenix Point), flanking pods, spawns. Some of that is resented (Mordheim's Rat Ogre). Without tactics, surprise depends on the pool: Frostpunk's scouts are called samey and "next to no randomisation"; Wildermyth critics start seeing "a box-of-attributes fed into an algorithm" once vignettes repeat. Whether a dealt event pool can stay surprising across a long campaign is unproven here. It is a content-volume question, and the v3 lesson (variety comes from dealt inputs) applies.

### P11. Spatial puzzle mastery and moment-to-moment improvisation. (a)
Into the Breach's telegraphed perfect-information puzzle, XCOM flanking/overwatch, JA's AP and interrupt play, Phoenix Point's free aim, Kenshi/RimWorld real-time rescues. This fun is genuinely tactical and does not survive conversion to dice. It is also, by the evidence above, not what most players retell from these games. Players who want depth for replay cite it (Wildermyth Steam).

### P12. Spend resolution time in proportion to stakes; keep the stretch between consequences short. (b)
Players auto-resolve obvious fights and go manual "when auto-resolve says that i lose a unit which i don´t want to lose." Battle Brothers players want autobattle only for won fights. Massive Chalice's long repetitive missions became a "meat grinder" between the bloodline beats players actually valued. *Implication:* routine trips resolve fast; slow down into roll-by-roll display when a named member's life is at stake.

---

## Bottom line for E-0002

The tactical layer in these games does two jobs: it is a puzzle (P11, tag a), and it makes losses feel owned (P4, tag c). Everything else players praise (choosing who goes, visible odds on a named person, push-your-luck, consequences that stick, failure that branches, earned attachment, relationships, stakes-scaled pacing) works with chance resolution, and in DD, Kenshi, RimWorld, Wildermyth, Massive Chalice and Mordheim it already does, at least partly. The non-negotiable for a dice deployment is **player input between rolls**. A roll chain with no choices is Frostpunk's weakest form, and E-0001 run 1 felt flat because it had none.
