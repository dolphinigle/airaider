# Auto-fights, lore checks and split minigames: what players and designers say

*Method note: research was cut short when the session's shared web-search budget ran out (about 30 searches plus about 25 direct page reads). Some claims rest on forum threads, not studies, and are marked as such. Several primary sources (PC Gamer, Pocket Tactics, Pelgrane, the Fractal Softworks blog) refused automated reads, so their claims come from search excerpts.*

---

## (a) Watching automatic fights and auto-resolve

### The common finding: agency comes before the fight
Players don't mind watching an automatic phase when they built the thing that plays out. Skeleton Code Machine, writing about the zero-agency board game *Magical Athlete*, says it most clearly: "agency isn't absent. It's front-loaded." The pleasure is "a different kind of fun — one in which we build a little system and see what it does." Without that investment, the same phase "would sound like a low-fun upkeep phase" ([Skeleton Code Machine](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/magical-athlete)).

The auto-battler genre is built the same way. Players "place characters... during a preparation phase," and those units then "fight... without any further direct input from the player" ([Wikipedia: Auto battler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auto_battler)). PC Gamer describes each battle as "a brief bout that you're only spectating, the result of which is determined by how strong your plan is" ([PC Gamer, via search excerpt](https://pcgamer.com/what-happened-to-autobattlers)). Teamfight Tactics' lead designer, in his GDC 2020 talk, summed up the aim as "tough choices, not touch execution." He listed "relaxed pace" as one of the genre's strengths. The team also cut "juggling" (micromanaging the bench), because it was stressful busywork rather than a real choice ([Pocket Tactics GDC writeup, via search excerpt](https://www.pockettactics.com/teamfight-tactics-design)).

### Loop Hero: praised for the cards, criticized for the watching
- **Origin.** The team literally "discussed the genre of 'Zero Player Games'" before inventing a hero who walks the loop by himself ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/?p=265570)). The lead designer's starting point was games where "you're not directly in control of your hero" ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/loop-hero-interview)).
- **Where the decisions are.** The player acts as a dungeon master: placing tiles and enemies to keep the hero challenged but alive, and choosing when to retreat. The hero "keeps pressing forward until told to retreat or dying" ([search summary of Gaming Nexus/PC Gamer](https://gamingnexus.com/Article/7480/Loop-Hero)). One critic said the game is "closer to a city-building sim or tower defense than a roguelike." In other words, the game is the world-building, not the fight.
- **Losing is softened on purpose.** "We didn't want players to feel afraid to experiment or punished for 'ineffective play'"; "we don't 'punish' players for losing." A dead hero still brings some resources home ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/?p=265570)).
- **The criticism is about the fight itself.** Ars Technica said the presentation screams "boring," then added "I cannot stop playing this game" ([Ars via Harvard TagTeam mirror](https://tagteam.harvard.edu/hub_feeds/3382/feed_items/2900969)). Others wrote: "combat encounters every 5 seconds with almost nothing interesting to look at" ([Attack of the Fanboy](https://attackofthefanboy.com/reviews/loop-hero-review/)); "the battles are on autopilot, and I wish I had more say" ([RAWG user](https://rawg.io/reviews/1295636)). A Steam thread opens with "a slow boring slog that basically plays for you." Its defenders answer that "the entire end of the game resolves around tactics, how to build your character, how to build your deck" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1282730/discussions/0/4768721792061467717)). Reviewers often call it a second-monitor game.

**Takeaway:** players tolerate the spectator part of Loop Hero because placing cards is constant, consequential decision-making. Nobody praises the fights themselves.

### Why some autobattlers hook people and others feel like spectator mode
From a Quarter to Three thread titled "Autobattler games – sit back and watch it" ([QT3](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/autobattler-games-sit-back-and-watch-it/165263)), anecdotal but specific:
- **Readability drives engagement.** One player prefers Mechabellum because "you can *see* what's attacking what and how well it's doing," which feeds the next decision. The opposite: Backpack Battles makes you "pore over a log for the various 'beats' of the battle"; another title's "fundamental problem is that runs are too long and battles are too opaque."
- **Burnout.** One player "got addicted to auto pets for a few days then burnt out." Another found a game "always looks rather samey."
- **Expression versus forced counters.** He Is Coming was criticized for focusing "on countering each upcoming boss rather than giving freedom of buildcraft."
- **Hard to watch from outside.** A Dota Underlords poster finds autobattlers less interesting to watch "because the skill cap is so low," and finds many units harder to follow than one character ([QT3 Underlords](https://forum.quartertothree.com/t/dota-underlords-valves-auto-chess/142504?page=10)).

### Auto-resolve in tactical games: wanted for trivial fights only
In Battle Brothers, players ask for "an autobattle feature (as opposed to autoskip)" for tedious mop-up fights once the outcome is clear. Others push back: "I hate the idea of an auto-win." Retreating is offered as the honest exit: "it will cost you a lot to heal your boys up" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/365360/discussions/0/135510194247672603)). Total War and Wartales discussions say the same: auto-resolve is welcome for repetitive or unimportant fights and resented when it takes away control over the ones that matter, or seems unfair ([TWCenter](https://www.twcenter.net/threads/auto-resolve.183986/), [Wartales Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1527950/discussions/0/3807279669983053310)).

### Darkest Dungeon: taking control away can work, if it produces character
Red Hook deliberately reduced player control. Afflicted heroes act on their own, because "people break in different ways" and the theme is "the willingness to fight." Four things make it read as story rather than spectating ([Game Developer deep dive](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/game-design-deep-dive-i-darkest-dungeon-s-i-affliction-system)):
- a visible stress meter, which they first wanted hidden but exposed so players have "a tangible thing" to manage;
- the 100-stress check staged as a tense reveal;
- short spoken lines ("barks") that announce the afflicted action before it happens;
- rare heroic reversals ("Virtues") and a persistent affliction history, building "a small emergent narrative for each hero."

The affliction system barely changed during Early Access ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/how-darkest-dungeon-got-inspiration-from-band-of-brothers-and-aliens)).

### Idle-game fatigue
The academic view is that idle games are "designed around the idea that the player will leave the game regularly," and that flexible play frequency is what prevents burnout ([NSF-hosted paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10188033)). Fatigue sets in when the loop turns into watching meters fill or compulsive checking with no decisions in between. Search turned up only weak opinion sources for this part. It matches the Loop Hero complaint ("plays for you") and the Super Auto Pets burnout-in-days story. Watching without deciding wears thin fast.

## (b) Pass/fail checks for knowledge, lore and social skills

### "It is never interesting to fail to get information"
GUMSHOE (Robin Laws, Pelgrane Press) skips the roll for investigative abilities. If you have the relevant skill and look in the right place, you get the core clue. Spending points buys extra, non-essential clues. Actions where failure can be dramatic, such as fleeing a monster, are still rolled ([Wikipedia: Gumshoe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gumshoe_System); [Pelgrane](https://pelgranepress.com/2020/08/17/additional-reasons-to-automatically-succeed/)).

Justin Alexander (The Alexandrian) disagrees: it "remove[s] the chokepoint of failure by simply removing the possibility of failure," and "Failing to find a clue is only ONE of the ways in which the clue can fail." His fix is redundancy: several paths to each clue. A commenter's rebuttal is worth keeping: combat is "dynamic and interactive," while a skill check is "a single roll with no feedback." GUMSHOE "doesn't remove the chance of failure, it just removes the chance of failure due to poor die rolls" ([Alexandrian](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/29334/roleplaying-games/thought-of-the-day-gumshoe-approach-to-clues)).

An old-school-revival (OSR) blogger calls the failed knowledge roll a "net zero": "Either you get what you want, or you wasted 10 seconds." His fixes:
- remove plain failure entirely;
- make a critical failure give *misleading* information, which adds bluffing;
- tie risk to the source: academy knowledge is reliable, tavern rumours are risky;
- make difficulties public, because known odds lead to "more interesting decisions"

([Random Ape Encounter](https://ape-encounter.bearblog.dev/recalling-knowledge-in-osrnsr-games/)). EN World threads add two more: failure can still deliver the information along with a complication, and stakes should be "transparent before the roll" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=7797049)).

### Disco Elysium: passive checks that reveal, hidden failures, failure written as content
- Passive checks fire automatically and add lines or options. Mostly flavour, sometimes key information ([wiki](https://breezewiki.discard.no/discoelysium/wiki/Skills)).
- Most passive failures are invisible: "most of the time you just don't know." Players think the game only tells you about near misses ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/632470/discussions/0/1755780148311380301)). One critic notes that a low-skill build never learns what it missed ([Backloggd](https://backloggd.com/u/ThatHikari/activity/friends/)).
- Design intent: "anticipating & designing interesting failure states are a key part of the design," and failures "update the state of the world in interesting ways." Red checks (one try, plot-critical) and white checks (retry after levelling up) create "scarcity-enforced choice." Before launch, when the game still had combat, "Skills... are always both combat and non-combat" ([RPG Codex](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=11127)). Some failures pay out, for example Conceptualization failures restore morale and give XP ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/632470/discussions/0/1609400247629680613)).
- Complaints concentrate on single-skill gates: "you have to pass one specific check with just one specific skill to move forward or else you're screwed" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/632470/discussions/0/1640927348811826300)).

### Baldur's Gate 3: the visible die and failure as content
The die tumbling on screen "has become one of the most talked-about hooks." The design line is "Failure is content, not punishment," with failure branches "written with the same care as the success path" ([CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/articles/baldurs-gate-3-redefined-crpg-dialogue/)). Player objections, from Steam threads: "you basically get punished for even making the attempt"; "combat is the default fail state to a lot of these checks"; and save-scumming makes the dice "a needless frustration" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1086940/discussions/0/2999920878439342239)).

### Knowledge as an unlock with judgment instead of a roll
- **Pentiment** has no knowledge rolls. Your background (medicine, law, theology) unlocks dialogue options, but "if you unlocked it, you can pick it, but sometimes it's not actually a good idea." Know-it-all options can hurt persuasion.
- **Fallout: New Vegas** used flat pass/fail checks. Sawyer's verdict: this "implied that if you ever saw your option come up, you should pick it," which hollowed out the role-play ([Feed4Gamers](https://www.feed4gamers.com/game-news/297795/josh-sawyer-reflects-on-design-evolution-from-icewind-dale-to-pentiment.htm)).

### Knowledge that feeds the fight
- **Pathfinder 2e Recall Knowledge.** Its main appeal: learning a weakness is "potentially 5-10 extra damage per round for every other character in the party." GMs report players "really like" choosing what to learn (weakness, weakest save, special ability) ([search excerpts: Know Direction](https://knowdirectionpodcast.com/?p=16744), [RPGBot](https://rpgbot.net/openvideo/v/pathfinder-2e-rewards-players-for-studying-monsters)). The dissent is about cost and redundancy. One GM's players "concluded the Recall Knowledge action was worthless/too expensive," because "just by attacking normally you get to know nearly everything." Defenders say "Recall Knowledge MUST be useful" and that it is "better as a third action than a third swing" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/rate-pathfinder-2e.668364/post-7908597)). One r/Pathfinder2e thread about a total party wipe blamed a check that revealed too much (an exact monster level) instead of something vague ([Reddit mirror](https://reddit.rtrace.io/r/Pathfinder2e/comments/1c9acdu/tpk_to_a_6_monster_how_could_we_have_run_away/l0nq9lo/?context=3)).
- **Darkest Dungeon curios** turn knowledge into an item. Using the right provision (a torch, a key) on a curio guarantees the good outcome, while bare hands gamble ([wiki](https://darkestdungeon.wiki.gg/wiki/Curio)). Red Hook said it dislikes that players need the wiki to know which item fits. Players split between experimenting and looking it up ([PC Gamer summary](https://www.pcgamer.com/uk/darkest-dungeon-to-make-curios-less-threatening-following-crimson-court-dlc)).

**Overall:** players like knowledge when it *changes the next decision or roll* (a weakness revealed, a guaranteed outcome, a new option). They resent it when it is a roll that either passes or wastes time, or a gate held by one skill.

## (c) Splitting the game into combat and non-combat minigames

### Griftlands: the clearest case study
Klei rebooted Griftlands because, in its own words, each system "felt disconnected with the rest" and actions felt "inconsequential." The reboot used two parallel card games, combat and negotiation ([Wikipedia](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Griftlands)). Reviewers:
- Negotiation was "far more interesting than the battles," which were "fun but conventional." But "committing to negotiation decks will punish you during the game's handful of inescapable combat encounters," so building for combat is the easier path. Negotiations reduce "every interaction into a simple binary of success or failure" ([PC Gamer, via search excerpt](https://pcgamer.com/griftlands-review)).
- It felt "almost like we're playing two different games at once." The payoff comes when you choose between paths with different consequences: arguing is "safer... but a limited commodity," persuasion is "less permanent... than killing," and killing hurts your reputation if anyone sees it ([GameCritics](https://gamecritics.com/mike-suskie/griftlands-review/)).

**Lesson:** a split works when the *choice between* the two modes carries consequences. It fails when the game forces one mode, so the other build becomes a trap, and when its outcome is a bare win or lose.

### Pillars of Eternity and Deadfire: separate skill pools and skill redundancy
- Pillars 1 kept only five non-combat skills "to make sure each skill got a lot of use, regardless of who had the skill," and no class has a monopoly on them ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k9f9h)). Sawyer says redundancy makes players feel they "made a dumb character." The goal was skills "useful to every character that invested in them," and he notes "Lore... is very broad. History or metaphysics or religion are much more specific" ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/kd64q)).
- Deadfire split skills into **active** (exploration and combat-adjacent) and **passive** (dialogue), divided Lore into History, Religion, Metaphysics and so on, and added party assists so companions contribute to the main character's checks ([Cubed3](https://www.cubed3.com/games/reviews/pc/pillars-of-eternity-ii-deadfire-2), [GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k4dfq)). One player complaint: "most of the time I'm either a little or a lot short of qualifying" ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/560130/discussions/0/2828702373000076963)). Narrow skills plus fixed thresholds mean frequent misses.
- Sawyer's principle: non-combat skills must "support the player's desired actions in the setting." ("How many Jedi pick locks? In my mind, zero.") ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/qa4u)).

### D&D: non-combat skills as a tax
The D&D Next "Avoiding Choice Traps" post proposed separate pools so that "Power Attack = Fast Hands = Ennui" never happens. Feats would cover combat, skills exploration and interaction. Most forum replies agreed. One poster observed that "the more the game rewards... non-combat activities, the more non-combat feats will be taken" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/d-d-next-blog-avoiding-choice-traps.322526/)). In a 4E thread, one side argued "useful less often, but vital when you are useful" counts as balance. The other replied that a character whose only value is a situational fix becomes a tiresome "NPC sage" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/threads/combat-non-combat-abilities-how-is-it-balanced.232272/)). A fighter's forced non-combat feat is literally called "a tax" ([EN World](https://www.enworld.org/goto/post?id=7550234)).

## Implications for airaider

**Fights**
- **Don't name a single weapon skill in a fight.** A fight test should name the *Fighting family*, and each merc brings their own best Fighting core: the axe-user brings Heavy Weapons, the archer Ranged. This follows Sawyer's "useful to every character that invested," and it makes the squad you sent change the result, which is the point of squad building.
- **If fights auto-resolve, put the decisions before and between rounds.** Before: who goes, gear, stance. Between short rounds: push, hold or retreat, with retreat costing wounds or loot (as in Battle Brothers and Loop Hero). A fight with no decision point is Loop Hero's "plays for you" without the card placement that made Loop Hero worth it.
- **Make the auto-fight readable as a few text beats, not a log.** Show three to five named beats per fight (who hit, who broke, who is bleeding), like Darkest Dungeon's barks. Players reject battles that are "too long and... too opaque" and hate having to "pore over a log." This also keeps fights playable as text.
- **Let mercs act out of character under pressure.** Stress or temperament can make a merc do something on their own, announced first by a one-line bark. Darkest Dungeon shows that taking control away reads as story, not spectating, when it comes from visible traits and is announced first.
- **Offer auto-resolve only for trivial fights.** Players want it for mop-ups. The fights that matter keep their decision points.

**Lore, talk and stealth checks**
- **Avoid net-zero lore tests.** A lore or knowledge success should feed the *next* test: reveal a weakness that adds coins to the fight, or unlock an approach. A failure should give vague or false information, or a cost, never "nothing happened." PF2e weakness reveals are popular because they raise the whole party's damage.
- **Consider auto-success for knowledge when the right merc is present.** Having the right core in the squad could grant the basic fact automatically (as in GUMSHOE), with the coin flip buying *extra*. Or the knowing merc could turn a gamble into a sure thing, like Darkest Dungeon's provisions.
- **Never gate a quest on one specific core.** Disco Elysium's single-skill gates and Griftlands' forced fights are the most common complaints. Every test needs at least two families that can attempt it, or a costly alternative path.

**Splitting combat and non-combat**
- **Keep one resolution system: coins for everything.** Two minigames made Griftlands feel like "two different games," and the weaker build became a trap. If fights get extra structure (rounds, retreat), build it out of the same coin flips.
- **Make talking versus fighting a choice with consequences.** Talk should be cheaper or safer but limited. Fighting should leave marks: wounds, reputation, loot. When a fight is forced, Talk and Knowing cores should still contribute, for example Intimidation breaking morale or Leadership steadying the line, so non-combat mercs aren't dead weight.
- **Watch the tax problem.** If combat stats are the only thing that keeps mercs alive, players will treat non-combat cores as a tax. Either give non-combat cores their own progression track (the D&D Next and Pillars split), or make them useful during fights.

**Presentation and failure**
- **Show odds and stakes before every flip, and show the flip.** Use public odds labels in the style of Fallen London's "chancy (60%)" ([FL wiki](https://fallenlondon.wiki/wiki/Broad_difficulty)), the BG3 tumbling die, and Darkest Dungeon's staged reveal. Show near misses rather than hiding failures.
- **Write failure as content.** A failed test should change the next scene: a complication, an injury, a false lead. BG3's "failure is content" is praised. "Punished for attempting" is the main complaint.
- **Use the coin flip as a hook.** A visible flip at moderate odds keeps players leaning in, while long watch-only phases drive burnout within days (the Super Auto Pets story). Keep the time from decision to flip short.
